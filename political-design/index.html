The grammar

Style is downstream of the printing press

The single most useful lens in this atlas: before you ask what a movement believed, ask what it could afford to reproduce. Almost every “look” below is a constraint that got rationalised into a philosophy afterwards.

Registers

Five ways type takes a side

All Power

THE SHOUT — condensed heavy sans, all caps, tight. Used by everyone from Constructivists to the Panthers, because it is the most words per square inch at maximum weight. Cheap, loud, ideologically promiscuous.

Vergangenheit

THE ANCESTRAL — blackletter and other “rooted” scripts, deployed to claim continuity with a real or invented past. Note that the Nazi state used Fraktur heavily, then banned it in 1941 on a false pretext and switched to modernist romans.

Sovereign

THE OFFICIAL — high-contrast display serif. The typography of proclamations, mastheads and constitutions. Signals permanence and, usually, that someone else wrote it.

Stencil

THE URGENT — cut-bridge stencil letterforms. Simultaneously the type of military crates and of graffiti, which is why it reads as both authority and defiance depending entirely on where it is sprayed.

Department of

THE NEUTRAL — plain grotesque, generously spaced. The most political typeface of all, because it works by disclaiming that it has a position. Every bureaucracy in this atlas is set in some version of it.

Method & ethics

How this atlas is made, and what it refuses to do

Everything here is drawn, not reproduced.

There is not a single photograph or scanned poster on this site. Every flag, emblem, banknote, ballot symbol and poster composition is redrawn as vector code from published descriptions and specifications, and labelled Recreation. That is partly a rights decision and mostly a method: you learn more about how a mark works by rebuilding it than by looking at it.

Regimes that committed atrocities are analysed, not styled.

Nazi Germany, Democratic Kampuchea and the Cultural Revolution have pages here because pretending their visual systems were incompetent is both false and useless. They were effective, and understanding the mechanism is the only defence against it. Those pages carry content warnings, state the crimes plainly, never reproduce victim photography, and never use the iconography decoratively.

“Good design” is not a moral category.

This atlas contains liberation movements and genocidal states using the same diagonal, the same flat red, the same condensed sans. Craft is available to anyone. The interesting question is never whether a piece of political design is good, but what it asks of the person looking at it — attention, obedience, or labour.

It is incomplete, and the gaps are real.

Fifteen movements is not a survey. There is no page yet for the Iranian revolution, Solidarność, ACT UP, the Zapatistas, Chile’s brigadas muralistas, the suffrage movement, or Hong Kong 2019 beyond a passing mention. Regional coverage is deliberately weighted towards South and Southeast Asia, because that is where the existing English-language design-history literature is thinnest.

Corrections welcome.

Where a detail is contested, the pages say so rather than picking a side. If something here is wrong — a date, an attribution, a flag construction, a transliteration — it should be fixed. Say so.
