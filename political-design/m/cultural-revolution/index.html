The Chinese Cultural Revolution

For ten years the visual output of a country of some eight hundred million people ran through one palette, one drawing style, one compositional doctrine and one face. It is the most complete design monoculture ever achieved at national scale — and the only one that wrote its style guide down and published it.

Content note

The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) produced mass persecution, the destruction of temples, libraries, artworks, instruments and private collections, and a death toll historians place at somewhere between several hundred thousand and several million. Millions more were denounced, beaten, imprisoned, or sent to the countryside.

This page analyses the visual programme that accompanied it: its palette, its written compositional rules, its objects, and how it was printed and distributed. The imagery is examined as a mechanism, not celebrated. Nothing here is reproduced from a photograph — every image on this page is drawn in code.

The anatomy

Six inks, and no alternatives

The palette is small, and it is small for two reasons at once. Half of it is doctrine — red is allocated, not chosen. The other half is manufacturing: a state offset press running two million sheets on absorbent wood-pulp stock has a limited number of well-behaved inks, and every one of them had to survive being pasted to a wall in a Manchurian winter.

A handwriting used as a wordmark

Chinese political typography of the period runs on three tracks that never mix: one man's brush hand for authority, drawn poster lettering for slogans, and imported Latin display for export. The first of those is the strange one — a personal script deployed as corporate identity across an entire country.

The devices

Four objects that carried a decade

The style guide

The rules were written down. Here they are.

Most house styles are inherited by osmosis and defended by taste. This one was published, taught, argued about in the cultural press and enforced by publishing houses. That is what makes the decade uniquely legible to a designer: you can read the specification, hold it against the artefacts, and watch it operate line by line.

I · Named doctrine

Rule 01 红光亮 hóng guāng liàng · "red, bright, shining"

Skin ruddy, light even and abundant, surfaces polished. No murk, no dusk, no weather. A grey passage anywhere in the picture is read as a grey position.

Rule 02 高大全 gāo dà quán · "tall, big, complete"

The hero is physically larger, placed higher, and morally finished on arrival. No development, no doubt, no interior. Character as a fixed quantity, drawn to scale.

Rule 03 三突出 sān tū chū · "the three prominences"
  1. 在所有人物中突出正面人物
    Among all characters, give prominence to the positive characters.
  2. 在正面人物中突出英雄人物
    Among the positive characters, give prominence to the heroic.
  3. 在英雄人物中突出主要英雄人物
    Among the heroic, give prominence to the central heroic figure.

II · The conventions that followed

Rule 04 The light Frontal, high, unmotivated

Heroes are lit from the front so that nothing crosses the face. Negative characters are lit from behind or not at all. Shadow is a moral instrument, not an optical one.

Rule 05 The camera Below the hero's chest

The viewer looks up. Heroes sit high in the frame and are seen slightly from beneath; negative characters sit lower, smaller, in profile, at the edge.

Rule 06 The colour rule Red is allocated, not chosen

Red marks the correct side and is never lent to the other one. Enemies are drawn in black, olive, sallow yellow and grey — a palette assigned by verdict.

Rule 07 No ambiguity One reading per face

Every expression must be legible at ten metres and admit exactly one interpretation. A face that can be read two ways is a production fault, not a subtlety.

Provenance of the rules: 三突出 was set out by the composer and cultural official Yu Huiyong in Wenhui Bao in May 1968, in the context of Jiang Qing's model works, and was subsequently tightened and promoted by Yao Wenyuan. 红光亮 and 高大全 circulated as shorthand — part description, part instruction — and were turned into terms of abuse after 1976. The conventions in section II are drawn from what the surviving posters consistently do, not from a single published document.

How it worked

Design monoculture is usually a figure of speech. Between 1966 and 1976 it was an administrative fact. The same red, the same contour, the same three-quarter frontal hero and the same radiant device appeared on posters, stamps, ticket stubs, enamel mugs, opera flats, textbook covers, factory gates and the fronts of buses, from Harbin to Kunming.

What makes the decade useful to a designer, rather than merely appalling, is that it left a paper trail. Somebody wrote the rules; somebody taught them; somebody enforced them at the publishing house. You can read the brief.

1964 · A book designed to be held up

毛主席语录 — Quotations from Chairman Mao Tse-tung — was compiled from the PLA Daily and issued to soldiers in 1964 by the army's General Political Department under Lin Biao. It reached general circulation in 1966 and then went into print runs counted in the hundreds of millions.

Read it as a product specification. Pocket format, sized to a tunic breast pocket. A red plastic-coated cover chosen because it survives rain, mud, sweat and a decade of being carried. Title blocked in gold. A few hundred short extracts, none longer than a paragraph, sorted into thirty-odd thematic chapters so that any situation could be met with a quotation in seconds.

Every one of those is a durability decision or a retrieval decision. None of them is a reading decision. The book's dominant use was display — raised in the right hand, spine outward, at head height — and in that position the object stops being a book. It becomes a red rectangle about ninety millimetres wide, and a crowd holding it is not a crowd of readers but a repeating module, tiled across a photograph.

That is a genuinely unusual brief, and it was solved well. This may be the only mass-produced book of the twentieth century whose governing constraint was how it photographs in someone else's hand.

1966 · The wall becomes a press

On 25 May 1966 Nie Yuanzi, a philosophy lecturer at Peking University, and six colleagues pasted up a 大字报 — a dazibao, a big-character poster — attacking the university's administration. Mao had it broadcast nationally on 1 June and printed in the People's Daily. On 5 August he wrote one himself: 炮打司令部, "Bombard the Headquarters".

As a medium the dazibao is close to perfect. Materials: ink, a brush, sheets of cheap paper, flour paste, a wall. Skills: the handwriting you already have. Production time: minutes. Capital cost: none. There is no press to seize, no plate to confiscate, no editor to arrest and no distribution to interdict, because you are the distribution and the wall is the front page. In an atlas full of movements improvising around a state's monopoly on printing, this is the purest zero-cost publishing medium in the book.

And it was, immediately and at enormous scale, the instrument of the persecution. The openness that makes the medium ungovernable is the same property that makes it unaccountable. A poster naming your neighbour, your teacher or your father as a class enemy cost the writer nothing, could not be corrected, and was read by everyone who walked past. Denunciation had found a format with no editorial layer and no cost of entry. Walls filled; people were beaten, dismissed, imprisoned, driven to suicide.

Both halves are true, and they are the same property described twice. The right to write dazibao was written into the 1975 constitution as one of the "four big rights", used again at the Xidan wall in 1978–79, and deleted by constitutional amendment in 1980. A state removed a right to publish because publishing had turned out to be uncontrollable — which is, viewed from the other side, the strongest available testimonial for the medium.

Uniformity here was not a side effect of the system. It was the specification.

1969 · The sun, billed in aluminium

红太阳 — the red sun. The device is simple: a radiant disc or a nimbus of rays positioned behind and above the head, the landscape pushed to the bottom of the frame, and the crowd arranged below in symmetrical adoration, looking up.

Every element of that is borrowed from devotional painting, and not loosely. The nimbus is a halo. The upward gaze is the posture of a donor figure. The symmetrical massing of followers is an altarpiece. The low viewpoint is the angle at which you see something mounted above an altar. Soviet poster art had already done this arithmetic — the sun and the radiating beam had been standing behind Stalin for two decades — but the Chinese version pushed it further, until the rays became the picture and the landscape was simply a surface for them to land on.

Then the identity programme escaped into hardware. Mao badges — stamped aluminium, portrait in relief on a sunburst ground, pin back, commonly two to five centimetres across, occasionally far larger — were produced from 1966 in quantities usually estimated in the billions. They were not centrally art-directed. Factories, schools, army units and work units designed and struck their own, competing on size, finish and invention, and thousands of variants survive. A totalitarian identity system produced its most prolific artefact through uncoordinated local manufacture, which is a strange sentence to be able to write.

It ended for a material reason. Aluminium is aircraft metal. By 1969 the badge programme was consuming enough of it to matter to the aviation industry; production was curtailed and badges were called back in, under an instruction popularly reported in the form "give me back my aeroplanes". An identity programme that has to be stopped because it is eating a strategic material is, whatever else it is, a measurable one.

1966–1976 · Authorship abolished

Cultural Revolution posters were largely made by studio collectives and published without individual credit — 集体创作, "collective creation" — through state houses such as the People's Fine Arts Publishing House in Beijing and its Shanghai counterpart, in editions running to millions of sheets, and distributed to workplaces, schools, barracks and homes. Amateur art groups in factories and communes were trained to reproduce the approved manner, with model works circulated as templates.

Consider what that does to a visual culture. Consistency becomes extraordinary. Iteration on an approved motif is fast, because nobody is protecting a personal style. Craft is real — these are competently drawn, well-registered, confidently coloured sheets, made by people who could draw.

What disappears is the channel through which dissent normally enters a visual culture, which is form. An artist who disagrees but cannot say so can still say it in the handling: a hesitation in the contour, a colour that sits wrong, a composition that refuses to resolve. Abolish authorship and you abolish that channel. There is no signature to be inconsistent with, no body of work to be in tension with, no personal manner failing to be suppressed. The style has no seams to work in.

That, and not the sheet count, is what makes this the most complete design monoculture in the atlas.

1976 · The style outlives the politics

Mao died on 9 September 1976; the Gang of Four were arrested on 6 October. Within a few years the aesthetic was officially repudiated, and 红光亮 and 高大全 became terms of abuse — shorthand for pictures that had been told what to look like.

Then it came back as material. From around 1990 Wang Guangyi's 大批判 (Great Criticism) series set worker, peasant and soldier figures drawn exactly to the old rules against the logotypes of Coca-Cola, Marlboro and Chanel — two identity systems that both believe in flat colour, heavy contour and total confidence, printed on top of each other. The critic Li Xianting named the tendency Political Pop; its companion, Cynical Realism, swapped the compulsory radiant expression for a compulsory grin.

Beneath that, a trade: reproduction posters, badges and books printed for tourist markets, and beyond it the drift of the whole language into a look — sunburst, raised fist, banner, hand-drawn slab — available for a gig poster, a hot-sauce label, or a political campaign that has never heard of Yu Huiyong.

This atlas keeps arriving at the same place. Cuban silkscreen became a t-shirt. Constructivism became a template. This became a filter. A style detached from its history is not neutral; it is a style whose one surviving argument is that history detaches. Worth naming here, because these rules were not decoration. They were written to make a certain kind of picture impossible, and they worked.
