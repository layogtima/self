Lakas ng Bayan

LABAN!People Power & the fall of Marcos

No. 14Southeast Asia · Philippines1983 — 1986

Four days in February 1986 removed a twenty-year dictatorship, and the identity that did it cost nothing. A colour from any haberdashery. A hand sign anyone could make. A song already in the charts. And confetti shredded from the phone book on your own desk.

The anatomy

This is deliberately the least designed page in the atlas. Nothing on it was commissioned, art-directed or specified. Every element of the People Power kit already existed as a consumer good, a pop song or an office consumable, and the movement's only creative act was to point at it.

A palette you could buy at the sari-sari store

There is no colour specification for People Power because there was no one to write it. The yellow is whatever yellow the ribbon shop had in stock, which is why it varies wildly across surviving material — and why it was impossible to control. A colour with no owner cannot be revoked.

Three registers, one of them a machine

Martial-law Philippines produced a strict typographic class system. The state got photo-typesetting and Helvetica. The opposition got a typewriter, a marker pen and the office copier after six o'clock.

Ninoy, hindi ka nag-iisa

Typewriter pica — 10 characters to the inch, single-strike ribbon, no way to set a headline. The mosquito press was written on portable Olivettis and Olympias and IBM office machines, then reproduced by whatever duplicator was to hand. "Ninoy, you are not alone" became the standard line after August 1983.

TAMA NA! SOBRA NA!

The placard register: a heavy condensed sans, hand-cut or drawn with a broad marker so it survives being photographed from forty metres. "Enough! Too much!" — the 1986 campaign line, usually completed by palitan na, "change it now".

Bagong Lipunan

The state's voice: the international modernist sans of late-1960s corporate identity, cut into travertine and applied to signage across the Cultural Center complex. Expensive, correct, and — this is the argument of the page — completely inert.

The marks

Four marks, and only one of them was ever printed

How it worked

Every other movement in this atlas built something. Constructivism had VKhUTEMAS. The Black Panthers had Emory Douglas and a weekly print run. Even the Khmer Rouge ran a propaganda ministry and a state magazine. People Power had a haberdashery, a radio station, a photocopier and a bin full of last year's telephone directories.

That is not a shortfall to be excused. It is the mechanism. Under Proclamation 1081 — martial law, declared 21 September 1972 — printing was licensable, presses were seizable and possession of the wrong leaflet was an offence. Any identity that depended on manufactured objects could be confiscated. So the movement built one out of objects that were already, legally, in everybody's hands.

1978 · The logo you make with your hand

Benigno "Ninoy" Aquino Jr. was in prison when he ran for the Interim Batasang Pambansa in April 1978. His Metro Manila slate was called Lakas ng Bayan — Strength of the Nation — which acronyms to LABAN, which in Tagalog means fight. The symbol was the letter L, made with thumb and forefinger.

Read it as identity work and it is close to perfect. It is free to reproduce at infinite scale. It requires no substrate, no ink, no press licence. It is simultaneously a letter, an initialism and an imperative verb, so the mark and the slogan are the same object. It cannot be confiscated, because it does not exist except while a person is making it — which also means every instance of the logo is a person publicly volunteering. And it photographs cleanly in a crowd, held above the head, in silhouette.

The 1978 campaign added the other found instrument. On the eve of the election, Manila was told to make noise at a fixed hour, and the city banged pots, pans, tin lids and car horns for a full evening. A mass demonstration conducted entirely with kitchenware, audible everywhere and photographable nowhere. LABAN lost every seat. The gesture survived the defeat by eight years.

1983 · The colour arrives pre-loaded

Aquino flew home from exile in the United States on 21 August 1983 and was shot dead on the tarmac at Manila International Airport, in custody, before he reached the terminal. The airport now carries his name.

In the weeks before the flight, supporters preparing to welcome him had settled on a device: yellow ribbons, tied along the route from the airport. The reference was Tie a Yellow Ribbon Round the Ole Oak Tree — Irwin Levine and L. Russell Brown's song, a number one for Tony Orlando and Dawn in 1973, about a man released from prison asking whether he is still wanted at home.

This is the sharpest borrowing in the story. No new symbol can arrive with meaning attached; it has to be taught, and teaching costs money and time and access to media, all three of which the opposition lacked. An off-the-shelf pop referent arrives already understood. Everyone within reach of a radio for the past decade already knew that a yellow ribbon meant a man coming home from prison and wanting to know if he was still welcome. The movement did not have to explain its colour. It had to supply ribbon.

After the assassination the meaning inverted without a single act of redesign: a ribbon that had meant welcome him back now meant they killed him. Two million people are estimated to have lined the funeral route on 31 August 1983. The colour went national inside a fortnight.

Bagong Lipunan had an anthem, a seal, a ministry and a national theatre in travertine. It lost to a length of ribbon that nobody designed.

1972—1986 · The mosquito press

The mainstream Philippine press under martial law was licensed, and largely held by people close to the Marcoses. What grew underneath it got called the mosquito press: small, irritating, impossible to swat completely. WE Forum and Malaya, both associated with the publisher José Burgos Jr., are the names that survived; WE Forum was raided and Burgos arrested in December 1982. Eugenia Duran Apostol's Mr & Ms Special Edition ran the assassination coverage the dailies would not, and in December 1985 she launched the Philippine Daily Inquirer into the middle of a snap election.

Below the mastheads sat an enormous unattributed layer: single-sheet leaflets, typed once and duplicated, with no author, no imprint and no date. Their aesthetic is a direct readout of their constraints. Monospaced body type, because a typewriter has one width. Hand-drawn or marker-drawn headlines, because a typewriter cannot set display. Ragged left-aligned setting with no margin discipline, because the sheet was composed in a machine, not on a board. Contrast blown to pure black and pure white, because the copy was already a copy of a copy.

The format itself carries an instruction. These sheets were designed to be copied again, by the reader, at their own office machine — single side, high contrast, no half-tones, nothing that degrades past legibility on the third pass. That is a design brief, and it is the opposite of the brief for a poster. A poster asks to be seen. A leaflet built like this asks to be duplicated.

The other channel was not visual at all. Radio Veritas, the Catholic broadcaster, carried Cardinal Jaime Sin's appeal on the night of 22 February 1986 asking Filipinos to go to Epifanio de los Santos Avenue and stand between the mutinying soldiers and the army. Its main transmitter was knocked out the next day; broadcasting continued from a smaller one, with June Keithley on air, under the name Radyo Bandido. An identity distributed by voice, with no graphics at all, assembling a crowd of hundreds of thousands in a few hours.

1972—1986 · The opponent had a real design programme

It is worth being precise about what People Power beat, because the usual telling flatters it. Bagong Lipunan — the New Society — was a genuine, funded, professionally executed state identity programme, and by the ordinary criteria of the discipline it was competent.

It had an anthem: Bagong Pagsilang, the New Society March, words by Levi Celerio to music by Felipe Padilla de León, taught in schools and played in cinemas. It had a party: Kilusang Bagong Lipunan, founded 1978, red. It had architecture at a scale no opposition could answer — Leandro Locsin, later National Artist, designed the Cultural Center of the Philippines (1969), the Folk Arts Theater (1974) and the Philippine International Convention Center (1976), a reclaimed-land complex of cantilevered travertine masses on Manila Bay. It had a patron, Imelda Marcos, and a slogan for her programme — the true, the good and the beautiful — and a building rate so aggressive that the phrase "edifice complex" entered Philippine usage to describe it. On the Manila Film Center, rushed to completion in 1981, a collapse during construction killed workers whose number was never established.

All of this was well made. None of it was usable. That is the whole finding. State branding of this kind asks for reception: look at the building, learn the anthem, recognise the seal. There is no verb in it for an ordinary person beyond attending and approving. The People Power kit had nothing but verbs — tie it, make the sign, shred it, throw it, sing it, stand there. A symbol you can perform recruits. A symbol you can only receive does not.

Add the practical asymmetry: the state's identity lived in objects that could be switched off, defunded, or simply walked away from, and every one of them was attached by name to one family. The opposition's identity lived in ribbon.

February 1986 · Four days, and a bin full of directories

The snap election of 7 February 1986 produced two declared winners and a walkout by computer technicians at the Commission on Elections who said the count was being manipulated. On 22 February the defence minister Juan Ponce Enrile and the constabulary chief Fidel Ramos broke with Marcos and barricaded themselves in Camps Aguinaldo and Crame. Sin went on Radio Veritas. Between then and 25 February, hundreds of thousands of people occupied EDSA.

The images that came off it are almost too neat: nuns kneeling with rosaries in front of armoured vehicles; women handing flowers and sandwiches to marines; Corazon Aquino in yellow, a widow in a colour taken from a song about a homecoming, functioning as a uniform so consistent that it worked as a party identity for the next thirty years.

And the confetti. From 1983 onward the Makati financial district had been holding rallies in which office workers threw paper from the tower windows, and what they threw is the detail that makes this page: shredded telephone directories, spent telex tape, and continuous computer printout torn into strips — the tractor-feed perforations still running down both edges. Not thrown paper standing in for confetti. Confetti manufactured on the spot, by the clerical staff of the financial sector, out of the waste stream of the financial sector.

Look at what that yields on a television screen. On one side, a grey-green column of armour on an eight-lane highway. On the other, an unarmed crowd in a single saturated colour, under a continuous fall of white paper, making a hand sign that reads at any focal length. Whether or not anyone planned it, this identity is optimised for a news camera: high chroma against neutral, dense repetition of one gesture, a moving foreground element that renders as light. Broadcast pictures of it were unanswerable, and they were the medium through which the outcome was decided.

After · Found symbols are cheap to turn around

The Marcoses left Malacañang on 25 February 1986, and went into exile in Hawaii. Yellow went with the winners: it became the colour of the Aquino family, then of the Liberal Party, then of an administration, then of an establishment. A colour that had signified everyone who is not the state spent twenty-five years becoming the colour of a particular faction's government.

By the 2010s "yellowtard" was circulating online as a routine slur for anyone associated with that faction — the same ribbon, re-pointed. This is the structural cost of the found symbol, and it is symmetrical with its advantage. A mark that costs nothing to adopt costs nothing to appropriate, invert or mock. There is no owner, no registration, no one with standing to object. Yellow could be captured by its enemies for exactly the same reason it could be adopted by its friends.

The Marcos family's rehabilitation ran on the identical logic in a different medium. Not monuments and anthems this time — that programme had already failed — but low-cost, high-volume, unattributed material distributed on platforms where anybody could copy it onward: the mosquito press model, executed by the side that once owned the presses. Ferdinand Marcos Jr. was elected president in 2022.

The lesson is not that improvised design beats professional design. It is that a symbol's distribution model decides its politics, and that the model does not care who is using it.

Artifacts

Four objects, none of them commissioned

A leaflet, a fistful of confetti, two page layouts and a piece of ribbon. Between them they are the entire production capacity of the winning side.

All four are drawn from published descriptions of martial-law-era ephemera, contemporaneous reporting and archive catalogue entries — not traced from photographs, and not reproductions of any single surviving document. The leaflet in particular is a composite: the format, register and instructions are typical, the wording is ours. For originals, see the archives in Sources.
