Monument, Banyan, Woodcut

No. 08Southeast Asia · Indonesia1945 — 1998

Three regimes in fifty-three years, each issuing a complete visual system. Sukarno poured his in concrete and gold. Suharto printed his in yellow — symmetrical, calm, an annual report of a country. In 1998 students carved the reply into plywood and carried it down the street.

The anatomy

Three palettes that never share a page

Indonesia is the rare case where you can date a graphic to within a decade by its colour alone. Each regime worked in a different material — cast concrete, printed stationery, carved plywood — and each material dictated what the colour could be.

Three ways of making a letter

None of the three lettering traditions here comes from a type foundry. One is cast and carved, one is bought from a catalogue, one is gouged out of wood with a chisel. The tool is visible in every case.

MERDEKA

Act I · Sign-painter's relief capital. Wide, even-weight, no thin strokes — because a thin stroke cannot be cast in concrete or read from the base of a plinth. No foundry credit survives for the Sukarno-era monument lettering; Rubik Mono One stands in for it here.

Pembangunan

Act II · The imported grotesque. The New Order arrived with the development decade and with its typography: dry-transfer sheets, IBM composers, condensed sans caps on village noticeboards, clean titling on state television. Archivo stands in.

Reformasi

Act III · Cut in negative. In cukil kayu the carver removes what will print white, so counters clog, strokes fatten and the baseline drifts. Its cousin is the brush-painted cloth banner. Permanent Marker stands in.

The marks

Four marks, three states

Akt I

MONUMEN

1945 — 1965 · Sukarno

A state that art-directed itself in cast concrete, bronze and gold leaf, and built every one of its landmarks to be photographed from below.

How it worked

Sukarno was an engineer by training — he took a degree in civil engineering at Bandung in 1926 — and he treated the capital as a commission. Between the handover of sovereignty in 1949 and his removal from power in the mid-1960s, Jakarta acquired a monument programme with a single, legible brief: make a country that has just stopped being a colony look like it was never one.

The style is the muscular socialist realism circulating everywhere in the non-aligned world at the time — heroic figures, forward stride, raised arm, broken chain. The delivery mechanism was the state: commissions, budgets, and a small number of sculptors, above all Edhi Sunarso, executing them.

1950 · A bird with a date in its feathers

The Garuda Pancasila was adopted on 11 February 1950, from a design credited to Sultan Hamid II of Pontianak and revised before it was approved. Read it as an identity system and the interesting decision is not the eagle — it is the counting.

Seventeen feathers on each wing, eight on the tail, nineteen and forty-five at the base and the neck: 17-8-1945, the date of the proclamation of independence. The mark cannot be redrawn without restating the founding claim, which makes it unusually resistant to the thing that happens to most state emblems — being simplified until it means nothing. The motto on the scroll, Bhinneka Tunggal Ika, is Old Javanese, lifted from the fourteenth-century Kakawin Sutasoma: unity in diversity, a phrase older than the state quoting it.

1961 · The pestle, the mortar and 50 kilos of gold

Two competitions, in 1955 and 1960, failed to produce a national monument Sukarno would accept. The executed design — a 132-metre marble-clad obelisk rising from a 45 × 45 metre plaza, opened to the public in 1975 — is credited to R. M. Soedarsono working with the architect Frederich Silaban. The official reading of the form is not "obelisk". It is alu dan lesung, the rice pestle and the mortar: a Javanese household object scaled to 132 metres, and simultaneously the lingga and yoni of Hindu-Javanese temple grammar.

That is a very deliberate piece of positioning. A modernist tower would have read as imported; a temple would have read as sectarian. The pestle-and-mortar reading gives the tallest object in the capital a domestic, agricultural, unmistakably Indonesian source — and then puts a gold-clad flame on top of it, visible from every approach road.

The same programme produced the Selamat Datang welcome statue for the 1962 Asian Games — two figures waving at arriving guests, sculpted by Edhi Sunarso from a sketch by the painter Henk Ngantung — the West Irian Liberation figure at Lapangan Banteng, a man bursting his shackles mid-shout, and the Gelora Bung Karno stadium with its great cantilevered roof ring. Every one of them is composed for an upward camera. The plinth is not a base; it is a lens.

Stated plainly

This act ends in mass killing. Following the killing of six army generals on 1 October 1965, the army and allied militias carried out large-scale killings of people identified as communists or their sympathisers; historians' estimates commonly range from around 500,000 to over a million dead between late 1965 and 1966, with hundreds of thousands more imprisoned for years without trial.

LEKRA — Lembaga Kebudayaan Rakyat, the Institute of People's Culture, founded in 1950 and by far the largest cultural organisation in the country — supplied much of the era's graphic labour: banners, murals, poster brigades, and the turun ke bawah method of sending artists to work alongside villagers. It was banned. Artists affiliated with it, including members of the Yogyakarta painters' collective Sanggar Bumi Tarung, were killed, imprisoned or exiled, and their work was removed from collections, exhibitions and curricula for three decades.

Akt II

Beringin

1966 — 1998 · The New Order

Thirty-two years of government designed as a corporate annual report: one colour, one tree, one ideology course, symmetrical margins and a great deal of white space.

How it worked

Uniformity, issued as stationery

Act I shouted from a plinth. Act II never raises its voice. It files, laminates, distributes and standardises — and that is precisely the horror of looking at it now.

The New Order's designers had a genuinely difficult problem and solved it with unnerving competence: how do you make a country of thousands of islands and hundreds of languages produce one legible surface? The answer was not a poster campaign. It was a system — colour, mark, uniform, syllabus, broadcast — applied at every administrative level down to the village noticeboard.

Nothing in it is thrilling to look at, and that is the point. Sukarno's graphics wanted your adrenaline. Suharto's want your compliance, and compliance is best requested in a calm sans-serif with generous leading.

1971

A tree that insisted it was not a party

Golkar — Golongan Karya, "functional groups" — contested its first election in 1971 and won. Its formal position was that it was not a political party at all but a federation of occupational groups, which mattered enormously in practice: civil servants were bound by monoloyalitas, a rule of single loyalty to the government, and could therefore not join a party — but could belong to Golkar.

The emblem does the same work as the constitutional fiction. Not a fist, not a star, not a wheel: a banyan, the tree that stands in the Javanese town square and shelters whoever sits beneath it. Passive, permanent, pre-political. In 1973 the remaining parties were forcibly merged into two, leaving exactly three contestants on the ballot paper for the next quarter-century.

Then the colour did the rest. Golkar yellow was applied not to posters but to infrastructure — fences, gateposts, kerbstones, guard huts, electricity poles, whole village streets repainted before a campaign. Indonesians coined a word for it: kuningisasi, "yellowisation". It is one of the very few political identities in this atlas that was rolled out with a paint roller rather than a press.

A state that wants uniformity does not need a great designer. It needs one colour, one form, and the authority to make everybody else use them.

1978

The ideology, delivered as coursework

In 1978 the People's Consultative Assembly adopted P4 — Pedoman Penghayatan dan Pengamalan Pancasila, the guideline for "internalising and implementing" the five state principles. It arrived as a curriculum: multi-day penataran courses, workbooks, standard diagrams, a fixed set of numbered points, sat by civil servants, teachers and students for two decades. By the mid-1980s organisations were required by law to adopt Pancasila as their sole ideological basis.

Around it, the rest of the system: batik promoted from regional textile to national formal dress, with long-sleeved batik as evening officialwear and a specific service-corps pattern for civil servants; the family-planning board's two-child household stencilled on kelurahan boards nationwide under the slogan Dua Anak Cukup; and, from 1976, the Palapa satellite — named after a fourteenth-century oath of unification — which for the first time made it technically possible for the whole archipelago to watch the same broadcast at the same moment.

Until 1989 there was exactly one channel to watch: TVRI, the state broadcaster, on air since the 1962 Asian Games. One national newscast, one anthem, one set of titling. A design brief most brand consultants would recognise instantly — total consistency across every touchpoint — executed with the powers of a state.

Stated plainly

Suharto's rule was authoritarian. It began in the 1965–66 mass killings, restricted electoral competition to three approved contestants, jailed critics under subversion laws, and censored the press through publication licences that could be withdrawn at will. Indonesia invaded East Timor in December 1975 and occupied it until 1999; the subsequent Timorese truth commission, CAVR, put the minimum number of conflict-related deaths there at around 102,800.

None of that is visible in the graphics. That is the finding, not an aside: this is the best-behaved design system in the atlas, and it belonged to one of the most repressive governments in it.

Akt III

Cukil Kayu

1998 · Reformasi

The currency collapses, the campuses empty into the street, and the visual language switches from print to plywood in a matter of weeks.

How it worked

Everything breaks

1998 · Twelve days in May

The Asian financial crisis took the rupiah from roughly 2,400 to the US dollar in mid-1997 to around 15,000 by early 1998. Prices for fuel and staples were raised in early May. On 12 May, four students were shot dead at Trisakti University in Jakarta. Between 13 and 15 May the city burned.

Stated plainly

The May 1998 riots included anti-Chinese pogroms. Ethnic Chinese homes and shops in Jakarta, Solo and elsewhere were looted and burned; more than a thousand people died, many trapped inside burning shopping centres; and the Indonesian government's own Joint Fact-Finding Team, reporting in October 1998, documented rapes and sexual assaults of ethnic Chinese women during the violence. Almost nobody has been prosecuted.

On 18 May students occupied the parliamentary complex — thousands of them, on the roof of the People's Consultative Assembly, in campus jackets. On 21 May, after thirty-two years, Suharto read out a resignation on television.

The uniform of those photographs was not designed for the occasion. Indonesian universities had been issuing coloured jaket almamater for decades, and the University of Indonesia's is yellow. The most reproduced image of the fall of Golkar's Indonesia is therefore a crowd in almost exactly Golkar's colour — an accident of institutional dress that reads, thirty years later, like art direction nobody signed off.

Everything else was made in the previous forty-eight hours. Bedsheets and cheap cotton spanduk cloth, poster paint, brush lettering by whoever in the faculty could letter; spray-can stencils; photocopied bulletins; and, very quickly, woodcut.

"Reformasi Total" — the demand on a thousand hand-painted banners, brush-lettered on cloth because cloth is what a campus can buy by the metre. Banner lettering · Jakarta, May 1998

December 1998 · A collective that refused to sign

Taring Padi formed in Yogyakarta on 21 December 1998, working out of the abandoned old campus of the Indonesian Institute of the Arts. Its chosen medium was cukil kayu — woodcut — and the choice was operational before it was aesthetic. A block needs no press, no electricity, no permit and no supplier. It can be printed by laying paper on the inked block and rubbing the back with a spoon. It can be printed directly onto cloth and carried. And it can be printed again tomorrow.

The formal language follows from the tool: hard black fields, white gouged lines, no halftone, no grey. Compositions are deliberately crowded — dozens of figures, farmers, workers, students, tools, banners within banners — because a woodcut has no depth of field and everything must be resolved at the same value. Faces are types, not portraits. Text is carved into the same block as the image, so lettering and drawing share one surface and one wobble.

The politics of authorship are carved in too. Works were made collectively, usually unsigned, without editions, and reproduction was encouraged rather than restricted. After the New Order's thirty-two years of licensed publication and single-channel broadcast, an image that anybody could copy was itself the argument.

A later note, kept here because this page is about how images travel: in 2022, at documenta fifteen in Kassel, a large 2002 Taring Padi banner, People's Justice, was covered and then removed after it was found to contain antisemitic caricatures. The collective apologised. An image made to be copied endlessly, by many hands, without a signature, is also an image nobody can recall.

Artifacts · the three acts side by side

Three alphabets, one country

Set the three key words on one shared baseline and the argument becomes a typographic one. Cast, printed, carved — and each one tells you exactly how much labour and how much permission it took to make a letter.

Every visual on this page is drawn in SVG from published descriptions, dimensions and documented conventions — none of it is traced from a photograph, and the woodcut panel is a composite in the 1998 manner rather than a reproduction of any particular print. Where a figure is uncertain (the weight of gold on the Monas flame, for instance) it is marked as reported. For originals, see the archives in Sources.
