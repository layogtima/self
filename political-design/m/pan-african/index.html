UHURU

Pan-African Liberation

No. 09Africa · continental1957 — 1980

Thirty-odd states in twenty-three years, and every one of them needed a flag, a coat of arms, banknotes, stamps, a passport and an airline tail-fin inside a year. It is the largest and fastest identity rollout ever attempted — executed by designers whose names we mostly did not bother to write down.

The anatomy

Four colours, two ancestries

The palette looks like one idea. It is two, arriving from different continents about eighty years apart, and the pages that flatten them into a single "Rasta stripe" get the whole history wrong. Red-black-green is Harlem, 1920. Green-gold-red is Addis Ababa. Ghana took bands from one and its star from the other, and that hybrid is what the rest of the continent copied.

The lettering is not folkloric, and that is the point

Look at the stamps and banknotes of 1957–1970 and you find almost no "African" display lettering. You find English sans-serifs and slabs, set tight, all-caps, on machines in London and Basingstoke. That was partly who held the presses — and partly a deliberate refusal to be exotic on your own money.

The marks

A star, two tricolours, a sunrise and a loom

How it worked

Between March 1957 and April 1980, more than thirty African states stopped being colonies and started being countries. Each one needed, almost immediately, a flag, a coat of arms, a currency, a stamp series, a passport, an anthem and — with surprising frequency — a national airline livery.

Count the deliverables and you are looking at the largest coordinated identity programme in the history of the discipline: hundreds of separate artefacts, drawn under extreme time pressure, on a continental scale, mostly between 1957 and 1968. Design history gives it roughly a paragraph. It gives the Olympic pictogram programmes of the same decade whole books.

1920 · Two flags, two genealogies, endlessly confused

The first ancestor is Harlem. In August 1920, at its convention in New York, Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association adopted a horizontal tricolour of red, black and green. Garvey connected it explicitly to a mocking American song of the period whose title ran Every Race Has a Flag but the Coon: the flag was a rebuttal, and it was designed to be one.

The second ancestor is Addis Ababa. Ethiopia's green, yellow and red belonged to a state that had defeated an invading European army at Adwa in 1896 and stayed off the colonial map. After Italy's 1935 invasion those three colours became, for Black internationalists everywhere, shorthand for a sovereignty that had never been surrendered.

They are not the same palette and they do not mean the same thing. One is diaspora, organised in America. One is a state, with an army. Ghana's flag in 1957 is the moment they fuse — Ethiopian bands, reordered, with Garvey's star laid on top — and after that the two lines are almost impossible to separate again.

1957 · Ghana goes first, and Theodosia Okoh draws the star

Theodosia Salome Okoh (1922–2019) was a teacher and painter when she entered the competition to design the flag of the state that would replace the Gold Coast on 6 March 1957. Her entry won. Red for those killed in the struggle for independence, gold for the country's mineral wealth, green for its forests and farms, and a black five-pointed star for African emancipation.

Name her, because the omission is the story. This is one of the very few national flags on earth whose designer is documented, living, photographed and on the record — and she is still routinely left out of the caption. She spent the rest of her life better known in Ghana for building the country's hockey association than for drawing the object on every government building in it.

The star was not invented for the flag. It came from Garvey's Black Star Line, the shipping company floated in 1919 to carry Black passengers and cargo across the Atlantic on Black-owned ships. The company collapsed. The mark did not. Nkrumah put it on the flag, then on the tail of Ghana Airways in 1958, then in the middle of Accra as the Black Star Gate at Independence Square in 1961 — the same shape at three scales, from a lapel to a piece of urban infrastructure.

That repetition is the whole method. Nkrumah's line — "Seek ye first the political kingdom, and all else shall be added unto it" — was matched by a visual programme that behaved as though the state had always existed: state publishing houses, a national broadcaster, a film corporation, a new currency, monumental concrete. And Nkrumah himself, arriving at the United Nations wrapped in kente, insisting that the uniform of a modern head of state could be handwoven cloth.

A flag, a banknote and an anthem can be delivered in ten months. Everything they promise takes longer than that, and some of it never arrives.

1960 · The state starter kit, and who actually printed it

Independence is, among other things, a procurement problem. The list is remarkably consistent from state to state: flag and construction sheet; coat of arms; banknotes in four or five denominations; a coinage; a definitive stamp series plus a commemorative independence issue; a passport; ministry letterheads and rubber stamps; police and army insignia; school textbooks; and, again and again, an airline.

Nearly all of it had to exist within a year, and almost none of the new states had a security press. So the work went where the plates already were. Crown Agents, in Millbank, continued to procure stamps and currency for member governments after independence exactly as it had before it. De La Rue, Bradbury Wilkinson, Waterlow & Sons and Harrison & Sons in High Wycombe kept the contracts. The intaglio dies for a country's first sovereign banknote were frequently cut in England.

Sit with that. The most emotionally loaded objects a new nation can issue — the money in your hand on the first morning, the stamp on the letter announcing it — were manufactured by the outgoing power's suppliers, to British security-printing standards, in British typefaces, with the printer's imprint in the sheet margin. Sovereignty arrived with a London address in eight-point type.

The counter-move was institutional rather than graphic. The West African Currency Board, which had issued one currency across four colonies since 1912, was dismantled and replaced by national central banks: the Bank of Ghana in 1957, the Central Bank of Nigeria in 1958. Where the print could not be repatriated, the authority to commission it was. And where a single state was too small to sustain a flag carrier, eleven francophone states simply shared one — Air Afrique, founded in 1961: a single livery for a bloc, which is a piece of Pan-Africanism you could actually board.

1962 · The movements had no presses, so they printed abroad

The new states are only half the story. Running alongside them, and lasting far longer, were the armed liberation movements: the FLN in Algeria; the PAIGC in Guinea-Bissau and Cape Verde under Amílcar Cabral; FRELIMO in Mozambique under Eduardo Mondlane; the MPLA in Angola; SWAPO in Namibia; the ANC in South Africa, whose black, green and gold had been in use since the 1920s.

Their iconography is tight and repetitive, which is what you want when your audience is illiterate in four languages and your medium is a mimeograph: the rising sun, the raised rifle, the hoe crossed with the book, the territory itself as a flat silhouette. Mozambique eventually put the hoe, the book and the AK-47 on its national flag and has kept them there. Angola's emblem — a machete crossed with a cogwheel — is a deliberate, unmistakable rhyme with the hammer and sickle.

But the movements had no printing capacity. So the posters were made elsewhere: silkscreened by OSPAAAL in Havana and folded into Tricontinental magazine, offset-printed by the Angola Comité in Amsterdam, produced by the Africa Groups in Sweden and the Nordic solidarity committees. This has a design consequence almost nobody states plainly. A poster made in Stockholm for a Swedish student audience is captioned in Swedish, printed in four colours on coated stock, and is asking for money. A leaflet run off in the bush for a village in Cabo Delgado is one colour on bad paper and is asking for recruits. They are different objects with different briefs — and the archives that survived, in Amsterdam and Uppsala, preserve mostly the first kind. What we think African liberation looked like is substantially what European solidarity looked like.

1967 · The hard part

This does not end with a flag rippling in slow motion. Several of these identity systems outlived the promises they were made for, and some were dismantled by the same states that commissioned them.

Ghana's own flag was edited twice for political reasons: the gold band was replaced with white in 1964 to match the ruling party's colours, and restored in 1966 after the coup that removed Nkrumah. The cedi, introduced in 1965 carrying his portrait, was withdrawn and reissued in 1967 without him. A currency recall as a purge — a whole population made to hand in the face.

In 1971 Mobutu renamed the Congo as Zaire and reissued everything: flag, currency, place names, and a dress code, the abacost, that banned the Western suit. It was rebranding at national scale, and it was reversed in 1997 by the people who overthrew him — the identity system going down with the government that ordered it.

And then Biafra. The eastern region of Nigeria seceded on 30 May 1967 with a complete state identity: a red, black and green flag carrying a golden rising half sun of eleven rays, banknotes issued by a Bank of Biafra from 1968, its own postage stamps, its own passports, its own overseas information offices. It functioned as a state for two and a half years and surrendered in January 1970, after a blockade produced a famine that killed a number of people — overwhelmingly children — that historians still put somewhere between several hundred thousand and more than a million. The photographs of that famine went on to shape the visual language of humanitarian fundraising for the rest of the century, mostly for the worse.

Say it plainly: a complete national identity can be designed, printed and distributed faster than a functioning state can be built. Biafra had a flag, a currency and a stamp catalogue, and it could not feed the people it had issued them to. The design was not the failure. But it is a useful and permanent warning about what design can and cannot certify.

Sources & further reading

  • Kwame Nkrumah, Africa Must Unite (1963) — the programme, in the words of the man who ran it.
  • Whitney Smith, Flags Through the Ages and Across the World (1975) — still the standard reference for flag construction, and unusually careful about attribution.
  • Frederick Cooper, Africa Since 1940: The Past of the Present (2002) — on what independence did and did not deliver.
  • Amílcar Cabral, Return to the Source: Selected Speeches (1973), including "National Liberation and Culture" — the clearest theory of culture-as-resistance written from inside a liberation movement.
  • Ghana Museums and Monuments Board and the Ghanaian national record on Theodosia Salome Okoh, designer of the flag of Ghana.
  • International Institute of Social History (IISH), Amsterdam — southern African liberation and solidarity poster collections, including the Angola Comité material.
  • Nordiska Afrikainstitutet (The Nordic Africa Institute), Uppsala — the Liberation Africa archives of the southern African liberation movements and the Nordic solidarity committees.
  • Lincoln Cushing / Docs Populi — documentation and cataloguing of OSPAAAL and international solidarity posters.
  • Doran H. Ross, Wrapped in Pride: Ghanaian Kente and African American Identity (UCLA Fowler Museum, 1998) — on narrow-strip weaving and its politics.
  • Elizabeth Harney, In Senghor's Shadow: Art, Politics, and the Avant-Garde in Senegal, 1960–1995 (2004) — a state-run design programme, examined closely.
  • The British Library Philatelic Collections — including Crown Agents material on colonial and post-independence stamp production.
  • Colin Legum, Pan-Africanism: A Short Political Guide (1962) — contemporary, and useful precisely because it does not yet know how any of it turns out.
