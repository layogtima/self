OSPAAAL & the Tricontinental

No. 12Americas · Cuba1966 — 1990

Between 1966 and 1990 a small office in Havana designed the visual identity of other people's revolutions — Vietnam, Angola, Palestine, Puerto Rico — silkscreened it in four screaming colours, folded it twice, and posted it to subscribers in some eighty countries.

The anatomy

A palette you can count

On a silkscreen every colour is a separate pull: a separate stencil, a separate pass through the frame, a separate drying rack. So a Cuban solidarity poster's palette is not a mood board. It is a budget, written in the open. Four colours means four pulls means four times the labour — which is why these posters are loud in exactly four or five voices and never in six.

Lettering drawn, not set

There was no comfortable route from a Havana type case to a screen. Display lettering was drawn by hand and cut straight into the stencil, which is why Cuban poster type is fat, closed, rounded and idiosyncratic — a letterform shaped by a scalpel and a squeegee rather than by a foundry. The small type, where it exists at all, is a plain grotesque doing bureaucratic work.

The devices

Four moves that do all the work

OSPAAAL's house style is not a set of images. It is a set of operations you can perform on any image, in any country, with a squeegee and four jars of ink.

How it worked

Nearly every movement in this atlas designed for itself. Cuba designed for everybody else. The Organisation of Solidarity with the Peoples of Africa, Asia and Latin America produced, for twenty-five years, a body of graphics about Vietnamese, Angolan, Palestinian, Guinean, South African and Puerto Rican struggles — and then, crucially, worked out how to get it out of the country.

The answer was a magazine and a postal service. That decision is the whole argument of this page, because it determined the format, the palette, the drawing, the typography and the afterlife of every poster OSPAAAL ever made.

January 1966 A conference that turned into a print shop

The Tricontinental Conference opened in Havana in January 1966: delegations from across Africa, Asia and Latin America, in a city that had been under a United States embargo for four years. Out of it came OSPAAAL, a permanent secretariat with a remit of solidarity across three continents.

Its most durable instrument turned out to be graphic design. Over roughly three decades OSPAAAL issued several hundred poster designs — a sustained output at a scale that would embarrass most design studios, produced by a revolutionary state with limited paper, limited ink and no interest in advertising its own products.

The Cuban context matters. After 1959 the new state absorbed its illustrators and painters into institutional design departments — the film institute ICAIC, Casa de las Américas, the party's own presses — and gave them subject matter instead of clients. OSPAAAL simply pointed that capacity outwards.

The fold Four creases and a mailing list

From 1967 OSPAAAL published Tricontinental magazine, and each issue carried a poster — folded down small enough to sit inside the pages and posted to subscribers in around eighty countries. A folded poster is the key formal fact of the entire programme, and its consequences run all the way down.

Consequence one: flat colour. A halftone screen cracks along a crease. Thousands of tiny dots sit on the ink surface, the fibre breaks, and the tone splits into a white line you can see from across a room. An unmodulated flat of screenprint ink is thicker, more elastic, and when it does crack it cracks into a hairline that reads as wear rather than damage. The poster was designed for the crease before it was designed for the wall.

Consequence two: composition. If a fold line is going to run through your artwork, you cannot put the meaning at the middle of the sheet. Cuban solidarity posters are built out of large shapes that survive being bisected — a silhouette, a field of colour, a ring of bands — and their type sits in a band at the foot, away from the horizontal creases.

Consequence three: scale. The sheet is sized to fold to magazine format, not to fill a hoarding. These are intimate posters. They were made to be unfolded in a bedsit in Milan or a dorm in Berkeley by one person holding them at arm's length.

Consequence four: distribution without a gallery. No exhibition, no dealer, no biennial. A subscription list and a post office got Cuban graphics onto student walls across Europe, North America, Africa and Japan, which makes these plausibly among the most widely distributed political posters ever made.

A poster that has been folded twice is no longer a poster. It is mail — and mail crosses borders that exhibitions do not.

There is a second design problem buried in the mailing list. Tricontinental appeared in Spanish, English, French and Arabic, and the posters carried their captions in the same four languages. Four scripts, wildly different set widths, one layout. French runs long; Arabic runs short but deep, and it reads the other way. No amount of tracking fixes that.

The solution was structural, and it is the smartest thing in the whole programme: make the image carry the message and let the type carry only a name. A silhouette and a colour field say what needs saying in any language. The four-line caption band at the foot then only has to hold a country, an organisation, a date. That is a design brief small enough to be solved four times over in a strip two centimetres deep.

The silkscreen look Flat, hot, and pulled by the artist

Serigraphy on cheap uncoated stock, short runs, one screen per colour, the designers often pulling their own editions. The technique is the aesthetic. You get pure unmodulated hues because that is what a stencil gives you. You get hard edges because a stencil has no other kind. You get no gradients except the split-fountain blends a printer can coax across a single pass, which is why those blends are always horizontal and always slightly different from sheet to sheet.

Out of that come the four moves catalogued above: the repeated silhouette in rotating colourways, the radiating concentric bands, the subject flattened to a single high-contrast shape, and the tolerated misregistration. Set beside Soviet Constructivism — which built the same reduction toolkit out of red, black and a diagonal — the Cuban version is warm, curved, tropical and frankly psychedelic. It is closer to Push Pin Studios, the Polish poster school and Op art than to anything in Moscow.

Name the designers, because the posters mostly do not: Alfredo Rostgaard, Olivio Martínez, René Mederos, Rafael Enríquez, Berta Abelénda, Faustino Pérez, Lázaro Abreu. Rostgaard's Cristo guerrillero — Christ with a rifle across his shoulder, blood-drop and all — is the most reproduced single image of the group's output after the Guevara material he also worked on. Mederos went to Vietnam and drew it, and the reportage that came back is the least formulaic work in the archive.

The Che pipeline How a photograph became a colourway

In March 1960 Alberto Korda, a Havana fashion photographer working for Revolución, made two frames of Guevara at a memorial service and filed them. Guerrillero Heroico sat effectively unused for seven years. In 1968 the Irish artist Jim Fitzpatrick turned it into the two-tone high-contrast poster that everyone actually knows — and the image left the building.

The mechanism deserves analysing rather than deploring. Threshold a photograph and you throw away every mid-tone; what remains is a shape. A shape can be cut into any stencil, at any size, in any two colours, by anyone, in any country, without a negative and without permission. Reduction does not merely simplify an image — it converts it from a photograph, which has an author and a subject, into a mark, which has neither. The politics are in the mid-tones, and the mid-tones are the first thing to go.

Cuban graphics of this period circulated without copyright notices, and were reprinted abroad freely and enthusiastically. Korda declined for decades to claim royalties on the most reproduced photograph of the twentieth century. He drew the line only in 2000, when he took legal action over the use of the portrait in an advertisement for vodka — a case he settled, on the argument that the image could be used for solidarity but not to sell alcohol. The distinction is honourable and, by then, unenforceable.

Afterlife The revolutionary graphic as merchandising template

OSPAAAL wound down in 2019. Long before that, its style had become a design-history commodity: reprinted in monographs, collected by museums, sampled by every gig poster and record sleeve that wanted to look insurgent for the length of one visual gag.

The clearest end point is Shepard Fairey's Hope of 2008. Look at it formally and there is no argument to have: a photographic portrait thresholded to four flat tones, in a hot restricted palette, distributed free as a downloadable file and adopted virally by people who printed it themselves. The most famous image of the Obama campaign is, in construction and in distribution model, a Tricontinental poster. The magazine has been replaced by a website; the fold has been replaced by a download. And the image, like Korda's, promptly became the subject of a copyright dispute — the Associated Press over the source photograph, settled in 2011.

That is what happens to a revolutionary graphic language once it works too well. Reduction was invented in Havana to solve a printing problem and a postage problem; it survives because it also solves a merchandising problem. A shape that can be recoloured infinitely can be resold infinitely. The technique that let Cuba give its posters away for nothing is the same technique that lets a t-shirt printer charge for them, and there is no formal difference between the two files.
