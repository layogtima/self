1933 — 1945

The Nazi Visual Machine

No. 06Europe · Germany1933 — 1945

Between 1933 and 1945 the German state ran the most totalising visual programme of the twentieth century: type, uniform, radio, architecture, film, ritual and the physical arrangement of crowds, built as one system by trained professionals. It was technically competent. It was infrastructure for genocide.

Content note · read before scrolling

The regime examined on this page murdered six million Jews. It murdered hundreds of thousands of Roma and Sinti; disabled people killed under a state medical programme; millions of Polish and Soviet civilians; some 3.3 million Soviet prisoners of war; gay men; Jehovah's Witnesses; and political prisoners of every description.

This page analyses that regime's visual apparatus as evidence — how mass persuasion was engineered, by whom, at what cost, and what it was used to do. Nothing here is admiration.

What this page does not show. The party emblem is not drawn anywhere on this page in any form — not rotated, not abstracted, not partially, not as a construction diagram, not in a typeface. Neither are the SS runes, the death's-head, or any other mark that operates today as a hate symbol. Where the flag is analysed, the field is drawn and the central device is replaced by a labelled grey box. That omission is deliberate, and it is part of the analysis rather than a gap in it.

Antisemitic caricature, slogans and propaganda text — including the material published by Der Stürmer — are named and examined here for their function. None of it is reproduced.

The anatomy

Note on this page's own layout Everything below sits on a rigid centred axis: symmetrical headings, vertical emphasis, heavy horizontal rules, a tight measure and large empty margins. That was the regime's compositional grammar, and it is used here deliberately so that you can feel the device working on you at the same moment you read its name. It is a demonstration, not a homage. Nothing on this page animates, glows, or reveals itself dramatically — the layout of the material it examines did all three, constantly, and that was the point of it.

Six colours, each one a specification

This palette was not a mood. It was issued. Colour marked organisation, rank and function across a state of eighty million people, and it had to survive night, distance, bad film stock and cheap printing. Every value below is doing an identifiable job.

Three typographies, one administration

The regime is usually remembered for blackletter. It spent its last four years suppressing blackletter. The third typography — the one nobody remembers — did most of the operational work.

Buchdruck

Fraktur and Schwabacher. Blackletter was promoted from 1933 as the authentically German letter, and foundries answered the demand with new cuts — Tannenberg (Erich Meyer, D. Stempel AG, 1933–35), National, Element: blackletter skeletons with hard mechanical joints. Withdrawn by order in 1941.

Buchdruck

Antiqua — the roman. The era's defining geometric roman was Futura (Paul Renner, Bauer, 1927); Renner was removed from his Munich teaching post in 1933 for opposing the party, and his typeface went on being set by state and export printers regardless. The tool held no politics. The man did.

Register · Card index · List

The typography that did the work: registration forms, index cards, transport manifests, punched cards. Dehomag, IBM's German subsidiary, leased Hollerith tabulators used in the 1933 and 1939 censuses and in state administration. The extent of that involvement is still argued over. The format is not: it looks like nothing at all, and that is what made it usable.

The instruments

Four machines for producing an audience

Not marks — apparatus. Each of these was designed, costed and manufactured, and each one converted a population into a controllable surface: light, sound, formation, ritual.

How it worked

Every other page in this atlas can be read as a movement assembling an identity. This one has to be read as a state assembling an environment. The difference is the whole finding.

In September 1933 the Reich Chamber of Culture made membership compulsory for anyone working as a designer, printer, photographer, architect, journalist, publisher or film-maker. Jewish and dissident practitioners were expelled from their trades. From that date the country's creative labour was licensed, and a licence could be withdrawn. What followed was not a campaign with a start and an end. It was an operating condition.

1934 · The total environment

A poster can be walked past. The structural innovation here was to remove the option — to build a field so saturated that there was no neutral surface left to stand on. Uniform, armband, banner, standard, marching column, ceremonial calendar, radio, newsreel and building were designed to reinforce one another, and the citizen moved through all of them in a single day.

The case study is Nuremberg. Albert Speer laid out party rally grounds across roughly eleven square kilometres; the Zeppelinfeld grandstand ran some 360 metres, its colonnade adapted from the Pergamon Altar in Berlin. Stone was slow, and the schedule was annual. So in 1936 Speer built the enclosure out of light instead: some 152 anti-aircraft searchlights, borrowed from the Luftwaffe, set roughly twelve metres apart around the field and aimed straight up. The beams were reported visible for several kilometres. A room with no ceiling and no material cost, erected in an evening.

Speer records in his memoirs that releasing that many searchlights was resisted, because they were a significant share of a strategic reserve, and that the objection was overruled on the argument that foreign observers would conclude Germany had them in abundance. If that account is accurate, the light show was simultaneously a stage set and a piece of strategic disinformation. Speer's memoirs are also a sustained act of self-exculpation and must be read against Gitta Sereny; he was the regime's architect, later its armaments minister, and was convicted at Nuremberg for the use of slave labour.

Radio was the same environment delivered indoors. The Volksempfänger of 1933 — one specification, dozens of manufacturers, 76 Reichsmarks, and a stripped 35-Reichsmark version by 1938 — put a receiver within reach of most households; by 1939 something like seventy per cent of German homes had one, reportedly the highest rate anywhere. The sets were not built to reach across a continent, and from September 1939 reaching across one was a crime with a card at the dial to say so.

The negative half of the environment was designed with equal care. In Munich in July 1937 the Entartete Kunst exhibition hung some 650 confiscated modern works deliberately badly — crowded, skewed, jammed together, with jeering slogans painted on the walls and purchase prices lettered beside them to enrage the taxpayer. It drew over two million visitors, considerably more than the regime's own approved art exhibition across the road. Exhibition design, used as a weapon, and more effective at drawing a crowd than the thing it was defending.

1933–45 · The professionals

The comfortable story is that this work was made by fanatical amateurs. It was not. It was made by the German creative profession, working to brief, for fees.

Ludwig Hohlwein had been the country's leading commercial poster designer since before the First World War — a master of the Sachplakat, the object poster, working in flat shapes, hard tonal edges and no outline, for shoe makers, coffee brands and Munich Zoo. His skill was entirely pre-existing. The regime rented it. Hans Schweitzer, signing as Mjölnir, produced a large share of the party's graphic output and was eventually made a Reich commissioner for artistic design — a designer with a state office and enforcement powers over other designers.

Leni Riefenstahl shot Triumph des Willens at the 1934 Nuremberg rally with around thirty cameras and a large crew, with pits dug for low angles, lifts fixed to flagpoles, and rails laid for tracking shots. In Olympia (1938) she put cameras below the runners, on rails beside the track, and underwater beneath the divers. Those techniques were not incidental to the argument; they were the argument, and they are standard in sports broadcasting today. She spent the rest of her life insisting she had made documentaries. Susan Sontag's 1975 essay "Fascinating Fascism" is the definitive demolition of that defence, and of the idea that you can admire the form and set the content aside.

Heinrich Hoffmann held an effective monopoly on photographs of Hitler and art-directed a public persona out of it, testing poses, controlling releases and profiting on a scale that would embarrass a modern licensing agency. Walter Maria Kersting designed the radio. Karl Diebitsch and Walter Heck designed the black SS uniform; the firm of Hugo Boss was among the manufacturers of party uniforms and used forced and prisoner labour during the war, which the company commissioned a historian to establish and acknowledged publicly in 2011.

And Herbert Bayer — Bauhaus master, inventor of the single-alphabet sans experiment, later one of the most influential corporate designers in America — stayed in Germany after 1933 and produced exhibition and brochure work for regime exhibitions before emigrating in 1938. That biography is not an aberration in the history of modernism. It is a fact in it.

Nothing here was made by cranks with no training. It was made by a country's professional creative class, on brief, on schedule, to standard. The finding of this page

1936 · The Olympics as a rebrand

The Berlin Games are the most successful reputation-laundering campaign in the history of the discipline, and they should be studied as such rather than as sport.

The identity was comprehensive in a way no previous Games had attempted: Werner March's stadium; an official poster by Franz Würbel; a bell cast at Bochum and inscribed with a summons to the youth of the world; a full pictorial system of sport pictograms, tickets, signage, medals and printed matter; the first television transmission of an Olympics, to public viewing rooms across Berlin; and Riefenstahl's two-part Olympia, released in 1938, which fixed the visual language of filmed athletics for the rest of the century.

And the torch relay, which Carl Diem invented for this event. A flame lit at Olympia by parabolic mirror, in a ceremony written in 1936 and performed by women in classical costume; 3,331 runners; roughly 3,187 kilometres; seven countries; twelve days; arrival timed to the opening. Krupp made the torches. Nothing about it is ancient, and almost everybody believes it is. It has been run before every Olympics since — a fascist invention permanently embedded in a global ritual, which is a harder fact to sit with than any poster.

The city itself was art-directed for the duration. Antisemitic signage came down. Der Stürmer street boxes were removed. And in July 1936, before the visitors arrived, Berlin's Roma and Sinti were rounded up and interned at Marzahn. The signage went back up after the closing ceremony. The internment did not end.

1941 · The Fraktur reversal

For eight years blackletter was the regime's proof of rootedness. Fraktur and Schwabacher were promoted as the authentic German letter; type foundries answered the demand with new cuts, including the slab-jointed blackletter-grotesque hybrids of the mid-thirties; schoolchildren were taught German cursive. Then, on 3 January 1941, a circular issued over Martin Bormann's signature by the Party Chancellery reversed all of it. Blackletter was declared to be "Schwabacher Jewish letters", and Antiqua — the roman — was designated the normal script of the Reich, to be adopted immediately across printing, administration, schools and export.

The stated justification is false, and no evidence was offered for it, because none exists. Schwabacher is a German type of the late fifteenth century, cut and used by German printers. The claim was manufactured in the same document that acted on it.

The real reasons were almost certainly logistical. By January 1941 German administration ran from Norway to the Pyrenees. Clerks, printers, signwriters and readers across occupied Europe did not read blackletter and could not set it. Material aimed at foreign audiences was illegible at a glance. German printing houses already held far more roman than blackletter. An empire that runs on paperwork needs one letterform that works everywhere, and it needed it at once.

This is the intellectual centre of the page. An ideology of blood and soil met the throughput requirements of an occupation, and the ideology was rewritten overnight — with a fabricated racial explanation bolted on afterwards so that the reversal could be presented as consistency rather than as a U-turn. The regime's own typographic mythology turned out to be entirely disposable the moment it cost administrative speed. Anyone who believes a totalitarian visual identity is held together by conviction should read the circular. It is held together by logistics.

Two further ironies deserve stating plainly. The letterform now made compulsory was the modernist roman whose leading practitioners the regime had already driven out or silenced — Renner removed from his post in 1933, the Bauhaus closed the same year. And the ban is the reason the regime's late material looks so much more modern than its early material: the visual break most people read as a change of taste was an administrative order.

1939–45 · What it was for

Everything above staged an in-group. All of it simultaneously marked an out-group, and the marking was operational rather than expressive.

From 1939 in occupied Poland, and from 19 September 1941 within Germany itself, Jewish people aged six and over were required by decree to wear a yellow badge on their outer clothing. It is not drawn on this page. Read it as workflow rather than symbolism: a legal category that existed on paper — in registers, census returns, ration files and card indexes — was moved onto the body, where any policeman, conductor, official, neighbour or informer could act on it instantly without asking for a document. Identification enables exclusion. Exclusion enables concentration. Concentration enables deportation. Deportation ended at Chełmno, Bełżec, Sobibór, Treblinka, Majdanek and Auschwitz-Birkenau.

The badge is a piece of interface design inside that sequence, and the tattooed number, the camp triangle and the transport manifest are the rest of it. This is why the design apparatus of this state cannot be described as decoration around a political programme. It was infrastructure for the programme. It performed identification, sorting and legitimation, which is precisely what an identity system is built to do.

Refuse the formalist reading, then, but refuse the comfortable one too. The techniques inventoried on this page — rigid symmetry, a saturated environment, one recognisable mark, a controlled palette, a total identity applied across every touchpoint, camera angles that flatter, a product cheap enough to reach every household — are not fascist techniques. There is nothing in a low camera angle that wants anybody dead. They are all in daily commercial use, in stadium broadcasts, brand guidelines, product launches and political campaigns of every persuasion, including entirely benign ones.

That is the uncomfortable point, and it is why this atlas keeps insisting that good design is not a moral category. Craft is a multiplier. What it multiplies is decided somewhere else, by someone else, and the designer's skill is silent on the question.

Afterlife · 1945 →

Denazification dismantled the institutions and missed most of the people. Section 86a of the German criminal code prohibits distributing or publicly using the symbols of unconstitutional organisations, with a carve-out for art, science, research, teaching and reporting — that clause is the legal space a page like this one occupies. Austria's Verbotsgesetz goes further.

The iconography did not die with the state that issued it; it circulates now in extremist movements across several continents, stripped of its history and treated as a shortcut to menace. That is the reason for the rules at the top of this page. Recognition is the entire mechanism of a mark. A design-history site that renders one cleanly, at high resolution, with the geometry helpfully explained, is not documenting an asset. It is manufacturing one.
