Three Fingers

No. 15Southeast Asia · Myanmar · Thailand · Hong Kong1988 — 2021

The last movement in this atlas is the first whose design system was never printed. It was assembled on phones — a salute lifted from a Hollywood film, a peacock inherited from 1936, a hashtag issued by Twitter, and a slogan translated overnight by strangers so that a foreign feed could read it.

The anatomy

Six colours, none of them mixed by hand

Every other palette in this atlas was constrained by what a press could hold: two inks, a paper stock, a dye that could be got locally. This one was constrained by what survives re-encoding. These are screen colours — saturated past what any lithographer would allow, because a photograph of a protest is going to be shot on a phone, compressed twice, screenshotted by someone else, and reposted at a quarter of its original size. Colours that hold up through that pipeline are colours that shout.

#0B0B0E OLEDNot black ink. Unlit pixels. The substrate of the whole movement is a phone held in the dark, and the interface it lives inside is dark by default.
#F04E23 FLAREThe NLD red pushed hot for screen. On paper it would look cheap; on an emissive display at 2am it is the only thing in the frame.
#D6202A CDM REDThe red of the NLD flag, reissued in February 2021 as a ribbon pinned to hospital scrubs. A party colour demoted to a wearable badge — cheaper, and harder to ban.
#F2B705 PEACOCKThe gold of the fighting peacock: a student-union mark from the 1930s that the NLD put on a red field in 1988 and never revised.
#FFD23F DUCKInflatable-toy yellow — and, awkwardly, the exact register of Thai royalist yellow. The 2020 protesters took the establishment's colour back in the form of a pool toy.
#C98A4B MILK TEAA brand colour derived from a drink. Three shades of tea-with-milk stood in for three territories, which is the least ideological colour scheme in this entire atlas.

Four scripts, one input method

Burmese, Thai, Chinese and English shared the same protest graphic — often the same image file. What unified them was not a typeface but a keyboard: everything here was set by non-designers in whatever the phone offered, and the design decisions that mattered were made about character counts and translation.

Burmese letterforms are round because the script was cut into palm leaf with a stylus: a straight stroke splits the leaf along the grain. A writing surface from the eleventh century dictating a glyph shape on a 2021 phone.

Thai, set loopless. Traditional Thai type carries a small head-loop (หัว) on most consonants; the loopless faces that dominate Thai UI and display work delete it, which makes the script behave like a Latin sans. Every protest graphic out of Bangkok in 2020 is loopless. The tradition is barely fifty years old and it now reads as the default.

The hashtag as a typographic form: no word spaces, so capitalisation does the spacing; no punctuation, because the platform strips it; and a mandatory leading glyph nobody designed. The marks

Four marks, and only one of them was designed

How it worked

Every previous entry in this atlas had a production bottleneck, and the bottleneck shaped the design. Someone owned a press, a silkscreen bed, a paint studio, a transmitter. That person made decisions, and those decisions are what we now call the movement's visual identity.

This one has no bottleneck. There is no art director, no printer, no commissioning committee and no approved file. What there is instead is a set of marks that were found rather than made — a student emblem from the 1930s, a gesture from a 2012 blockbuster, a pool toy, a drink — and then versioned in public by thousands of people at once. The design question is not "who drew this". It is "why did this spread and not something better".

1988 · The fighting peacock

On 8 August 1988 — 8/8/88, a date chosen by astrologers and by graphic designers simultaneously — Burma's cities filled with people demanding the end of Ne Win's one-party state. Over the following six weeks the army shot its way back into control; the death toll is generally put in the thousands, and it has never been established. On 18 September a new junta, SLORC, took over.

The emblem the students carried was not new, and that was the point. The ခွပ်ဒေါင်း — the fighting peacock — had been the mark of the Rangoon University Students' Union since the anti-colonial agitations of the 1920s and 1930s. A dancing peacock had sat on Konbaung-era coinage and on the colonial-era flag badge before that. The 1936 student strike used it; Aung San's generation used it; Ne Win dynamited the student union building in 1962 and the peacock survived the demolition because it was never in the building.

Read as strategy, inheriting a mark is the opposite move to inventing one. An invented mark has to be taught, and teaching takes distribution you do not have under censorship. An inherited mark arrives pre-loaded: it says we are the continuation of the people your father shot at, and it says it to the state as clearly as to the street. The National League for Democracy, founded in 1988, put a golden fighting peacock and a white star on a red field and has not revised it since.

The NLD's other mark was a face. Aung San Suu Kyi — daughter of the assassinated independence leader — became the movement's portrait, which is an efficient and dangerous piece of identity design: efficient because a face needs no legend, dangerous because a face has a biography that keeps running. Her international standing collapsed after 2017, when the Myanmar military's operations against the Rohingya in Rakhine State drew findings of genocidal intent from the UN's Independent International Fact-Finding Mission, and she defended the state at the International Court of Justice in 2019. The portrait outlived the politics it was drawn to stand for. Marks based on people always carry that risk; almost nobody prices it in.

A logo nobody owns cannot be seized, licensed, priced or discontinued. It also cannot be defended, corrected or withdrawn.

2014 · The borrowed salute

On 22 May 2014 the Royal Thai Army, under General Prayut Chan-o-cha, took power for the thirteenth time since 1932. Public assembly of more than five people was banned. Within days people at bus stops, in shopping malls and outside cinemas began raising three fingers — index, middle, ring — and the junta announced that doing so could get you arrested.

The gesture came from The Hunger Games, released in 2012, where it is a District 12 salute of thanks and farewell. Nobody in Bangkok designed it. What they did was select it, and the selection is the design work.

Look at the specification it satisfies. It costs nothing and requires no object, so possession of it is not evidence. It is instantaneous — a placard takes a second to raise and a second to see; a hand takes neither. It is legible in a low-resolution photograph from any angle where the hand is visible, which matters because the photograph is the actual deliverable. It is silent, so it works in a mall. And its meaning was pre-installed: an entire generation had already watched the film, so the gesture arrived with a story about a rigged system and a televised spectacle already attached to it, at zero distribution cost to the movement.

The Philippine LABAN "L", thirty-six years earlier, solved the identical brief with the identical mechanism — a hand sign, free, mass-manufacturable by bodies, banned and therefore amplified. The difference is the source of the pre-installed meaning. LABAN's came from a party name and a jailed man; the three fingers' came from a Hollywood franchise owned by a studio in Santa Monica. That is not a decline in seriousness. It is a change in where a generation's shared reference set is stored.

2020 · Ducks, hamsters and dinosaurs

The backdrop is a decade of Thai politics conducted through shirt colour. From 2005 the yellow shirts — the People's Alliance for Democracy — wore the colour associated with King Bhumibol's birth day. From 2007 the red shirts of the UDD wore the opposite. A country in which political allegiance was a garment: instantly legible, instantly sortable, and impossible to take off at a checkpoint. The 2010 crackdown on the red shirt encampment in central Bangkok killed roughly ninety people.

The 2020 student movement did not pick a shirt. It picked mascots, and it picked them out of fandom. In July and August 2020 protesters ran laps around the Democracy Monument chanting a rewritten Hamtaro theme — the children's cartoon hamster whose original lyric names the tastiest thing in the world, amended on the spot to the people's tax money. On 3 August a rally was staged in Harry Potter costume, complete with a speech about the one who must not be named; it was, in practice, the first mass public speech about monarchy reform. The older generation were dinosaurs, the students were the meteorite. In November, when police used water cannon at parliament, inflatable rubber ducks appeared as improvised shields and were mascots within hours.

The design logic here is not whimsy, it is a threat model. An absurdist mascot is cheap and already in the shops. It cannot be trademarked into a single owner the state can arrest. It is very hard to prosecute — the charge sheet has to say the words "rubber duck" out loud. It is impossible to counter-brand, because the state has no register in which a joke about a pool toy can be answered. And most importantly it changes what a photograph of violence looks like: a riot policeman advancing on a placard is enforcing order, and a riot policeman advancing on a two-metre yellow duck is a picture that does the movement's argument for it. This is image management, executed by teenagers, in real time.

April 2020 · An alliance named after a drink

The Milk Tea Alliance began as a fight about nothing. In April 2020 a Thai actor and his girlfriend were attacked by Chinese nationalist accounts over posts read as treating Hong Kong and Taiwan as separate places. Thai Twitter, comprehensively outnumbered, responded with jokes. Hong Kong and Taiwanese accounts joined in. Within days the thing had a name, and the name was a beverage: Thailand, Hong Kong and Taiwan all drink their tea with milk, and mainland Chinese tea is drunk without.

As an identity system this is close to a limit case. The membership test is a drink. The palette is three shades of the same drink. There is no manifesto, no membership, no office, no founder, and — this is the extraordinary part — the logo was issued by a corporation. In April 2021, on the alliance's first anniversary, Twitter released an official #MilkTeaAlliance hashtag emoji: a platform-drawn mark, auto-appended to every use of the tag, rendered at 16 pixels, distributed globally in a day.

Every movement in this atlas before this one had to solve distribution. This one had distribution handed to it by a company with its own shareholders, terms of service and enforcement policy — and by 2023 the same platform had changed hands, changed name and changed its rules. A logo you do not host is a logo you are renting.

February 2021 · Designed to be screenshotted

On 1 February 2021 the Tatmadaw detained Aung San Suu Kyi and President Win Myint, annulled the November 2020 election result and installed Min Aung Hlaing. The three-finger salute crossed the border from Thailand within days — not as a metaphor, as a literal import, carried by the same Twitter accounts that had spent the previous year in the Milk Tea Alliance.

Three design responses followed, and each has a different intended viewer. The nightly banging of pots and pans, from 2 February onward, is aimed inward: it is a traditional act for driving out evil, it needs no organiser, it is audible from inside a locked flat, and it lets a whole street count itself. The Civil Disobedience Movement — begun by hospital staff walking out — took a red ribbon and the three-finger salute and built an identity out of two objects already in every home.

The third response is the one that should be studied. From the first week, hand-painted placards in Yangon and Mandalay were written in English. Not for the soldiers, who read Burmese. Not for the neighbours. For the feed — #WhatsHappeningInMyanmar — and for the wire photographers whose images would be cropped to a square. So the signs were lettered large, high-contrast, short enough to survive a crop, and phrased in a register that a stranger scrolling in another country could parse in half a second. When a nineteen-year-old named Kyal Sin was shot in Mandalay on 3 March 2021, the photograph that travelled showed her in a black T-shirt reading Everything will be OK. English, on a body, in a Burmese street. Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International documented the security forces' use of battlefield weapons against these crowds through that spring.

Designing past the local authority to an international audience is not new — the Salt March was staged for newsreel cameras in 1930. What is new is the latency. The newsreel took weeks and was edited by a studio. The screenshot takes four seconds and is edited by nobody, which means the protester is now also the art director, the copywriter and the picture editor, standing in the street, choosing a font size.

The mechanism · What unownable costs

It is tempting to end an atlas on the movement that finally escaped the printing press. Resist it. A shared identity that is gestural, memetic and cross-border has one structural weakness, and it follows directly from its strength: nobody owns it, so nobody can control it.

There is no authority that can say a given use is not the movement. The salute has since been raised by people the 2014 protesters would not recognise, and by people using it to mean something else entirely. A hashtag can be flooded by anyone with a keyboard; state accounts have done exactly that. A mascot can be sold as a T-shirt by a company with no stake in the outcome. An alliance defined by a drink has no way to expel anyone, because there is nothing to be expelled from. And a movement whose distribution runs on one platform inherits every decision that platform's owner makes.

Myanmar's junta still holds power. Thailand's 2020 movement produced prosecutions, not reform. That is not a verdict on the design — no salute was ever going to out-mass an army. But it is worth writing down plainly, at the end of a book about how movements looked, that being unforgettable and being effective are different specifications, and this system was optimised hard for the first one.

Not a metaphor. This is the actual page layout the design was made for: a column roughly 390 pixels wide, scrolled with a thumb, in a dark interface, at night. Every formal decision in this movement's graphics is downstream of that rectangle.

01

Square, or it gets cropped

Timelines crop wide images. Placards were lettered to survive a centre crop — short lines, big weight, nothing important near an edge.

02

Legible at 40% scale

The image is seen twice: once full, once as a thumbnail in someone's quote-post. Condensed grotesques and one accent colour survive both.

03

Bilingual by default

Burmese for the street, English for the feed, stacked in one frame. The translation is part of the composition, not an afterthought.

04

Degrades gracefully

Every repost re-encodes. A design that depends on fine detail dies at the third screenshot. A hand, a duck and a hashtag do not.
