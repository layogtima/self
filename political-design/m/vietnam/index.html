TRANH CỔ ĐỘNG

Agitation painting

Vietnamese Poster Art, Painted One at a Time

No. 07Southeast Asia · Vietnam1945 — 1975

There were almost no presses, so the Democratic Republic of Vietnam painted its propaganda: gouache on rough paper, a handful of copies per design, rolled up and carried out to a village, a factory or the front. Flat colour, hard edge, no gradient. The method is the style.

The anatomy

Five tins and a black line

A tranh cổ động palette is not a mood board, it is an inventory. Poster colour came in jars, was mixed by hand in the atelier, and had to cover in one pass on paper that drank it. Every colour below does a structural job; none of them is decorative.

The marks

A vocabulary of four shapes, repeated for thirty years

How it worked

Every propaganda system is shaped by its printing plant. Vietnam's had almost none. Between 1945 and 1975 the north fought two wars on a paper supply that was rationed, imported and periodically bombed, and its answer was to treat the poster as a painting: one artist, one brush, one sheet at a time.

The result is a body of work that behaves like graphic design — flat colour, closed contour, a fixed cast of symbols, a house palette — while being produced like folk art. That contradiction is the whole subject.

WHAT IT IS · Agitation painting

Tranh cổ động translates roughly as agitation painting: tranh is a picture, cổ động is to rouse or mobilise. The state ran poster ateliers under the culture apparatus. An artist was given a slogan and a deadline and painted the thing in gouache or poster colour on whatever stock existed. Where a silkscreen frame or a small offset press was available, a design might be pulled in a few hundred. Where it was not — which was often — a design existed in five copies, or one, and the copies were not identical.

Look at what that does to the drawing. Unmodulated colour, because a flat field is fast, dries even, and does not betray a rough sheet. Heavy black contour, because the outline is the part that has to survive being copied by somebody else. Compositions assembled from four or five separable shapes, because that is what a second painter can rebuild from a written description and a memory.

The Constructivism page in this atlas makes the identical argument from the opposite end: Soviet form came out of a lithographic press with two inks and a deadline. Vietnamese form came out of no press at all. In both cases the reproduction method wrote the style sheet, and the ideology turned up afterwards to take the credit.

1945–54 · A flag, a stencil, a mountain

The August Revolution of 1945 and Hồ Chí Minh's declaration of independence on 2 September gave the Việt Minh a flag: red field, yellow five-pointed star, nothing else. It is the most economical national mark in this atlas. One colour, one shape, one geometric rule — the star's circumscribed circle is a fifth of the flag's height. In 1955 the National Assembly straightened the star's arms, which had been fuller and softer in the earlier drawings. A revolution correcting its own kerning.

The graphics of the first war were stencil and woodblock: cut once, pull many, no press required. Tô Ngọc Vân — trained at the École des Beaux-Arts de l'Indochine, founded in Hanoi in 1925 by Victor Tardieu — ran a resistance art class in the Việt Bắc hills from 1950. He was killed in 1954, on the way back from Điện Biên Phủ.

Hold on to that detail, because it changes how you read the flatness. These were French-academy-trained oil, silk and lacquer painters. Nguyễn Sáng, who drew the young republic's first stamps and banknotes, could render anything he wanted. The flatness was a decision about reproduction, not a shortfall of skill.

A printed poster is a thousand identical objects. A painted one is a single object that has to be worth the walk out to the village.

1966 · Twenty-five letters that will not fit

On 17 July 1966 Hồ Chí Minh issued an appeal containing the sentence Không có gì quý hơn độc lập tự do — nothing is more precious than independence and freedom. It became the most-lettered string of text in Vietnamese political design, and as display copy it is genuinely hostile.

Count it. Twenty-five letters, nine words, marks on nine of the letters, three of which carry two marks at once. Set it on one line and it is too long to read at fifty metres. Break it into three and the stacked marks force the lines apart until the block loses the density that made it a block. Nearly every solution in the archive is the same solution: compressed caps, three or four short lines, marks flattened and pulled down close to the letter, and the leading measured from the top of the tone marks rather than from the cap height.

TYPOGRAPHY · Two marks on one letter

Quốc ngữ — the national script — is the Latin alphabet doing a job it was not built for. Portuguese, Italian and French missionaries romanised Vietnamese in the seventeenth century; Alexandre de Rhodes' Dictionarium Annamiticum Lusitanum et Latinum of 1651 fixed much of the convention, and the colonial administration made it official, displacing chữ Nôm and Chinese characters by the early twentieth century.

The alphabet then carries two independent layers of information at once. Seven letters are modified in their own right — ă â ê ô ơ ư đ — and five diacritics mark tone: huyền, sắc, hỏi, ngã, nặng. Four of those sit above the vowel. Nặng is a dot below it. Which means a single capital can be a three-decker: Ệ is an E with a circumflex above and a dot underneath.

For a letterer working at poster scale this is the entire job. The marks are the difference between two different words, so they cannot be dropped, shrunk into decoration or tucked inside the counter. But every mark above the cap line steals leading, and every dot below eats the space of the line beneath. The conventions the ateliers arrive at are consistent and genuinely clever: caps compressed horizontally to buy width, tone marks drawn flatter and closer than in text setting, circumflexes widened so they read as a shape rather than a speck, and vertical rhythm set mark-top to mark-top. It is display type design carried out by painters with no foundry to argue with, and it is almost entirely undocumented in the English-language design literature.

THE ICONOGRAPHY · The standing cast

The vocabulary is small and it repeats: the yellow star; the conical nón lá; a rifle crossed with a sheaf of rice; the raised fist; a factory chimney and a paddy field inside the same frame; a production figure set in numerals as large as the slogan. And, constantly, a woman holding a rifle — not as an allegory or an exception, but as an ordinary subject, drawn at the same scale and with the same authority as the men beside her.

Put these next to the neighbouring traditions and the differences are formal, not merely political. Cultural Revolution posters are modelled: airbrushed skin, ruddy cheeks, an implied sun, and very often Mao's face as the compositional anchor. Soviet work of the same decade leans photographic or heroically sculptural. Vietnamese posters stay flat. Hồ Chí Minh appears, but far less often than you would predict, and usually as one figure among others rather than as the light source.

Some of that is doctrine. Some of it is arithmetic. A modelled face needs a painter who can render and time to render in, and a hundred posters a month does not have either. Flatness scales; chiaroscuro does not.

THE OTHER SIDE · Paper as ordnance

The Republic of Vietnam and its American backers fought the other half of this graphic war, and they fought it by volume. The Chiêu Hồi — Open Arms — programme, launched in 1963, invited fighters to defect; the Joint United States Public Affairs Office in Saigon wrote and designed the leaflets, and aircraft dropped them in the billions. By unit count it is one of the largest print runs in the history of graphic design, and essentially none of it is in the design canon.

The safe-conduct pass is the object worth studying. It is a leaflet designed to be picked up, believed, hidden on a body for weeks, and then produced in front of an armed stranger without getting its carrier killed. That brief has four requirements that fight each other: official enough to be credible, generic enough not to incriminate, tough enough to survive folding into a wet pocket, and legible to a soldier on the other side who has to recognise it in about one second. The answers are visible on the artifact — bordered like a banknote, allied flags in a row along the top as a signature, the same sentence set in Vietnamese, English and Korean so that whoever finds you can read it, and the whole thing sized to a hand.

Close to two hundred thousand people were recorded as ralliers across the programme's decade. How much of that the paper did is unknowable, and the honest position is to say so rather than to credit the design.

AFTER 1975 · The idiom outlives the emergency

After April 1975 the style walked straight into peacetime administration: literacy, hygiene, family planning, traffic safety, forest fires, and later HIV. Same flat fields, same contour, same raised arm — pointing at a helmet instead of a rifle. Đổi Mới in 1986 opened the economy, commercial print came with it, and the hand-painted poster stopped being the cheapest way to say anything.

What happened next is the ending this atlas keeps arriving at. The idiom became merchandise. Walk the Old Quarter in Hà Nội and the posters are for sale as reproductions — on paper, on canvas, on lacquer, on tea towels — priced for tourists and increasingly redrawn by people who have never used the vocabulary in anger. Havana's OSPAAAL prints went the same way, and so did the Constructivist canon. An aesthetic engineered to be free, urgent and disposable ends up expensive, decorative and permanent.
