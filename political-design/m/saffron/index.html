Saffron, the Lotus & the Ballot Symbol

No. 04South Asia · India1925 — present

Two Indian design systems, one page. A movement that took a flag as its guru rather than a man, and grew into the loudest poster idiom in the country. And a state pictogram alphabet, drawn anonymously in 1951, so that a hundred and seventy million people who could not read could still vote.

Note on this page

The first half of this page analyses the visual system of Hindu nationalism, a movement whose organisations and campaigns have been implicated in episodes of communal violence in India — including violence documented along and after the route of the 1990 Rath Yatra, and the demolition of the Babri Masjid in 1992.

Its symbols are reproduced here as evidence and analysed as mechanism. Showing a mark is not endorsing it.

The second half covers a separate and unrelated design system: the Election Commission of India's ballot symbols, a state project with its own designers, constraints and history. The two appear together because one eventually ends up inside the other.

The anatomy

Six colours, one of them a range

Saffron has no published specification in this movement. There is no equivalent of IS 1, no Pantone chip, no brand book — and that turns out to be the point. The identity is a hue band between orange and red-lead, which means a screen-printed banner, a polyester flag, a temple gatepost and a phone screen can all be "correct". It survives reproduction because it was never pinned down.

Three kinds of lettering, none of them typeset

The striking thing about the movement's typography is how little of it is typography. Banner Devanagari is painted. Hoarding Latin is a vinyl-cutter's all-caps grotesque. And since about 2014 the dominant carrier is a JPEG in which the type is not set at all — it is baked into the picture.

Text rasterised into the artwork

The 2014-onward decision. Type is flattened into the image so it cannot be cropped, stripped, re-set or auto-translated in transit. Every re-share re-encodes it. Legibility is therefore designed against compression, not against distance — heavy strokes, high contrast, no hairlines.

The marks

A flag, a lamp, a flower and a set of clothes

How it worked

Hindu nationalism is usually discussed as ideology and rarely as art direction. Read as design, it is one of the most methodical identity programmes of the twentieth century — and its founding decision was to put a graphic mark, not a person, at the centre of the organisation.

What follows tracks that mark through four states: a flag, a uniform, a ballot symbol, and a vehicle.

1925 · An organisation that made a flag its guru

The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh was founded in Nagpur in 1925 by K. B. Hedgewar. Within a few years it had settled a question most organisations never even ask: what, exactly, do members owe allegiance to? The answer was the भगवा ध्वज — the saffron flag. Not the founder, not a text, not a deity. The flag itself is treated as the guru, and the annual offering ceremony is made to it.

This is a genuinely unusual identity decision and it has consequences that are structural, not sentimental. A movement organised around a living leader has a succession problem and a portrait problem: the mark must be redrawn every generation. A movement organised around a mark has neither. The flag can be raised in ten thousand neighbourhood grounds simultaneously, costs almost nothing to make, and cannot be discredited by anything a human being does.

It also makes the flag load-bearing in a way that a logo normally is not. The swallow-tailed silhouette — two points at the fly, the upper one longer — is the whole asset. There is no wordmark, no monogram, no secondary lockup. The organisation's entire visual equity sits in one silhouette and one hue band.

Most organisations put a person at the centre and arrange a mark around them. This one put the mark at the centre and left the place where the person goes deliberately empty.

The uniform · Khaki shorts, and the rebrand of 2016

The गणवेश — the RSS uniform — is the most copied piece of political costume design in India, and it is almost entirely derived from the thing it opposed. White shirt, khaki shorts, black cap, brown leather belt, and a lathi: this is colonial police and cadet-corps issue, reassembled. The drill that goes with it — formation, marching, staff exercise, a fixed daily hour on a neighbourhood ground — is drill in the imperial sense.

Borrowing the coloniser's kit was not an accident of availability. A uniform of this kind does three things at once: it makes an ordinary man in an ordinary street legible as a member of something; it is cheap and locally tailorable; and it silently claims the authority of the state's own disciplinary iconography. Anti-colonial movements that adopted khadi were arguing about production. This one was arguing about order.

Then, in 2016, the shorts went. They were formally replaced with brown full trousers, with a stated rationale about the uniform's appeal to younger recruits — the first change of that magnitude in ninety-one years. Strip away the politics and it is a textbook identity refresh: a single component of a famous system, retired because the component had aged badly while the system had not. The colour moved from khaki to brown. Every other element was left untouched, which is exactly what a competent rebrand does.

1951 → 1980 · The lamp goes out, the lotus opens

The Bharatiya Jana Sangh, founded in 1951 under Syama Prasad Mookerjee, contested elections under the दीपक — an earthen oil lamp. As a ballot mark it is a difficult object. A diya is a shallow bowl plus a flame, and at a thumbnail size, printed in one colour on cheap paper, the flame collapses into a blob and the bowl reads as a boat or a shoe. It is beautiful and it does not scale.

When the Bharatiya Janata Party was founded in 1980, the mark became the lotus. Every property the lamp lacked, the lotus has. It is radially symmetric, so it cannot be printed upside down. Its silhouette survives at 8 millimetres. It is built from a repeated element, so a bad drawing is still recognisably the same thing. And it fills a square, which matters when your mark sits in a ruled box on a ballot paper next to thirty others.

The party flag pairs it with green — saffron above, green below, lotus across the join. That pairing lets a party mark quote the national tricolour without copying it. But the lotus's real double duty is the one this page ends on: it is India's national flower, a religious symbol in more than one tradition, and an item on a list maintained by the Election Commission.

1990 · Set design at national scale

On 25 September 1990, L. K. Advani left Somnath in Gujarat in a vehicle that had been dressed to look like a chariot. Under the fibreglass it was a DCM Toyota light commercial vehicle. Over it went a moulded shell: ornamental wheels bolted to the flanks, a stepped canopy on the roof, a scalloped skirt along the sill, a saffron pennant on a mast, and a public-address system.

This is not poster design. It is set construction and touring production — a single built object moved through a country on a published schedule, arriving at towns where a crowd, a stage and an amplifier were waiting. The design brief was not "make an image"; it was "make an object that generates the same image in three hundred different places, for six weeks, at no marginal cost per impression."

Judged purely as art direction it is the most consequential thing in this half of the page. It also has to be described accurately: communal riots occurred along and after its route in several states, with substantial loss of life. Advani was arrested at Samastipur in Bihar on 23 October 1990 and the yatra was halted before it reached Ayodhya. Two years later the Babri Masjid was demolished. The vehicle did not cause any of this. It was the instrument that made a campaign simultaneously visible in places that had no television and no newspaper reach — which is precisely the design achievement, and precisely the problem.

The idiom · A poster optimised for its channel

The visual language that grew around all this is instantly recognisable and almost never analysed on its own terms. Airbrushed gradients. Saffron drop shadows under saffron objects. Lens flare where no lens exists. Cut-out heads floating at three-quarter scale over a crowd rendered as texture. Text in two or three languages stacked until the margin runs out.

Every one of those choices is a channel decision. The airbrush and the flare come straight out of Indian chromolithographic calendar art, the devotional prints Christopher Pinney and Kajri Jain have documented as the country's real mass visual infrastructure — a distribution network that already reached every home, and which political imagery simply merged into.

After 2014 the channel changed and the idiom changed with it. The dominant carrier became a compressed image forwarded phone to phone: high saturation to survive re-encoding, heavy strokes because hairlines die in a JPEG, and — the decisive move — text rasterised into the artwork rather than set beside it, so that the message cannot be cropped away, translated away, or separated from the picture in transit.

That is a design system built around a reproduction constraint, and it is worth naming the comparison honestly: it is the same kind of thinking that produced Soviet constructivist posters designed for two-colour presses. Same engineering logic, entirely different politics. Nothing about the constraint transfers the ideology.

Part two · a different design system entirely

Now the page changes register. Everything below this line is state design: a pictogram alphabet commissioned by the Election Commission of India so that people who could not read could still cast a vote.

It has nothing to do with the movement above it. It is one of the great uncredited public-interest design projects of the twentieth century, and it is almost never discussed as design at all. The two halves of this page meet in exactly one place, and that place is at the very end.

Form · Part two Election Commission of India 1951 —

How the state solved it

A pictogram alphabet for one hundred and seventy-three million people

In 1950 India appointed Sukumar Sen, an ICS officer and a mathematician, as its first Chief Election Commissioner. He was handed a problem with no precedent anywhere: run a general election, on universal adult franchise, across a country that had just been partitioned, with roughly 173 million people on the rolls, a large majority of whom could not read.

Ramachandra Guha's account of the 1951–52 election in India After Gandhi is the standard telling, and it is largely an account of logistics: rolls compiled from scratch, ballot boxes manufactured in the millions, polling stations established in places with no roads. But sitting inside that logistics problem is a pure information-design problem, and the solution to it is the subject of this half of the page.

1951 · One box per candidate

The solution was to give every candidate a pictorial symbol, and then to remove reading from the act of voting altogether. In the first general election the voter was not asked to mark a name. Each candidate had a separate ballot box, and each box carried that candidate's symbol. You took a blank ballot paper and put it in the box with your candidate's picture on it.

Consider what that removes. No name recognition. No alphabetical order effect. No need to match a printed string to a remembered one. The only cognitive operation required is object recognition — the single most robust visual skill a human has, and one that is completely independent of schooling. It is, as an interaction design, close to perfect, and it was arrived at in about eighteen months by people who were not designers and had no word for what they were doing.

It was also enormously expensive in hardware, and from 1962 the system moved to a single ballot paper with every candidate's symbol printed in a ruled row. The symbol survived the change. The box did not.

1968 · The symbol list becomes a system

The Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968 turned an improvisation into an administered alphabet. Recognised parties get reserved symbols, held permanently and nationally. Everyone else draws from a published pool of free symbols, allotted on application, constituency by constituency. The Commission maintains, updates and publishes both lists, and it draws the artwork in-house.

Look at what is actually on the list: hand, lotus, bicycle, elephant, broom, clock, ceiling fan, ladder, coconut, table, cot, whistle, arrow. These are not arbitrary. They are almost entirely household and agricultural objects, and the selection criteria are legible in the objects themselves.

  1. 01 Instantly nameable — and nameable in dozens of languages, because the symbol has to be spoken about at a doorstep, not just seen on paper.
  2. 02 Distinguishable in one colour, at thumbnail size, on bad paper, printed by whichever press won the district contract.
  3. 03 Rotationally unambiguous. A symbol on a ballot in a badly lit booth may be seen at any angle. A ladder is a ladder upside down; a lamp is not.
  4. 04 Silhouette-first. Every one of these objects survives being reduced to a solid black shape — which is how they are printed.
  5. 05 Not obscene, not a national symbol or emblem, and not something that could be confused with one.
  6. 06 And — in principle — carrying no religious or communal connotation.

That is a design brief, written by a state, for a pictogram set that has now been in continuous public use for more than seventy years across an electorate that has grown past 900 million. Otl Aicher's Munich pictograms of 1972 are in every design history. This system predates them by two decades, operates at a hundred times the scale, and its designers are not named anywhere.

Design a system to be neutral and you have not removed the question of what its symbols mean. You have only moved that question to the allotment desk.

The tension · A neutral alphabet must still allocate a lotus

Here is where the two halves of this page meet. The lotus is a reserved symbol of the Bharatiya Janata Party. It is also India's national flower, and a religious symbol of long standing in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain iconography. It sits on a state-issued ballot paper, drawn by the Election Commission, in the same flat black line as the bicycle and the ceiling fan.

There is a recurring public argument in India about religiously resonant symbols on state ballots — about the lotus, and at various times about others. The Commission's position rests on the objects themselves: a flower is a flower, a hand is a hand, and the Order excludes symbols with religious connotation, not objects that also appear in religious contexts. That is a coherent position and it is not obviously wrong.

But the design observation stands on its own. A pictogram system achieves neutrality by rendering everything identically — same weight, same box, same ink, no hierarchy. It cannot control what an object already means outside the box. The system flattens the drawing. It cannot flatten the referent. And so the most rigorously neutral graphic instrument the Indian state ever built spends every election day hosting a symbol that arrives with meanings the instrument was designed to exclude.

Both of those things are true at once. That is the whole page.
