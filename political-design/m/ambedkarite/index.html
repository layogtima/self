Every other identity in this atlas was made by people the state could already see. This one was assembled by people it had spent centuries refusing to seat — and so it chose the most conservative instruments available: a barrister's suit, a drafted wheel, and the one colour nobody else had claimed.

§ 01 — The anatomy

A palette chosen by elimination

By the 1940s the Indian colour map was fully allocated. Congress held saffron, white and green. The communists held red. The Muslim League held green with a crescent. Hindu nationalism was already annexing saffron on its own account. Ambedkar's Scheduled Castes Federation took the one wavelength still lying around, and then found reasons for it afterwards — which is how most good identities actually happen.

Three registers, and they never mix

Almost every other movement here has one voice. This one runs three in parallel, each doing a different job: the Marathi press voice, the letterhead voice, and the wall voice. Their separation is the discipline.

Art. 17 — "Untouchability" is abolished

The bureaucratic register. A movement whose founding victory was a clause in a document inherits the typography of documents: numbered articles, quotation marks around the word itself, monospaced tabulation. Its opposite is the cyclostyled stencil of the little magazines — the same ideas, run off on a drum.

§ 03 — How it worked

Respectability is usually the enemy of political design. Movements rough themselves up: they want the woodcut, the smudge, the handmade look that proves authenticity. Ambedkarite design does the reverse, and it does it deliberately. Straight rules. Set type. A tailored suit. Precision is not decoration here — it is the claim.

The reasoning is simple once stated. Caste had already assigned Dalits the visual attributes of dirt, roughness and improvisation. Adopting the aesthetics of the handmade would have confirmed the assignment. So the movement took the grammar of the professional class instead — law, print, drafting — and used it as a rebuttal you can read from across a street.

1927 · A counter-ritual, staged and photographed

On 20 March 1927 Ambedkar led thousands of Dalits to the Chavdar tank at Mahad in the Konkan and drank from it. The tank had been declared public by municipal resolution; the resolution had changed nothing. The march made the gap between the document and the ground visible in a single gesture.

Later that year, at the follow-up conference in December, a copy of the Manusmriti — the text underwriting caste order — was burned on a prepared pyre in front of the assembled delegates. Read as design, this is not a riot. It is a ceremony: a platform, a programme, a stage-managed act with an agreed script, witnesses, and press coverage in Ambedkar's own Marathi fortnightly, Bahishkrit Bharat, launched the same year.

The movement understood from the start that an event without documentation is a rumour. Every subsequent set piece — the conversion at Nagpur, the annual gatherings at Chaityabhoomi — follows the same production logic.

Gandhi took clothes off to stand with the poor. Ambedkar put clothes on because his people had been forbidden them. Both were design decisions, and they are incompatible.

The suit · The most consistent element in the entire iconography

Look at a thousand Ambedkar images and one thing never varies. Western three-piece suit, usually blue. Necktie. Round wire spectacles. A bound volume under the left arm. Occasionally a fountain pen. In eight decades of reproduction this has drifted less than any logo in this atlas.

Caste is, among other things, a dress code: it specified what fabric, what footwear, what ornament each rank could wear, and enforced it violently. A three-piece suit is therefore not aspiration or Anglophilia. It is a refusal executed in worsted wool, and it is legible in a fifth of a second, which is faster than any slogan.

The suit also encodes the credentials — Columbia, the London School of Economics, the Bar at Gray's Inn — that the caste order held to be impossible. The book completes the sentence. Set the suited jurist beside Gandhi in a loincloth at the 1931 Round Table Conference and you have two entirely opposed theories of what an Indian leader should look like, photographed on the same afternoon.

Blue · The last free wavelength

The explanation offered inside the movement is the sky: it covers everyone and has no boundaries, unlike land, which caste divides. The second explanation is nīla, the first band of the Buddhist flag — retrofitted neatly after 1956. The third explanation is the honest one. Red belonged to the communists, saffron to Hindu nationalism, green to the Muslim League, and the tricolour to Congress. Blue was the only slot open.

What matters is the discipline that followed. The Scheduled Castes Federation flag and later the Republican Party of India flag are plain blue with a white wheel — no crest, no lettering, no gradient. Its restraint is exactly the point: a field of solid colour reads at a distance, prints on anything, and is almost impossible to get wrong at a village press.

1956 · An entire visual religion, acquired in one afternoon

At Nagpur on 14 October 1956, Ambedkar and a crowd reported in the hundreds of thousands took the refuges and his own twenty-two vows, and converted to Buddhism. He died seven weeks later, on 6 December.

The design consequence was immediate and total. The movement acquired, in an afternoon, a colour system (the five bands), a mark (the wheel), a garment (white for the laity, ochre for monks), an architecture (the stupa, and later the domed monument at Deekshabhoomi modelled on Sanchi), and a pilgrimage calendar. No agency could have delivered that scope of identity. Conversion did it as a by-product.

It also solved a problem no logo could. Buddhism gave the movement a claim on Indian antiquity that did not run through the caste order — an inheritance older than the thing it was refusing.

The statues · A strict identity system with no head office

Tens of thousands of Ambedkar statues stand in Indian villages, and the specification is close to fixed. Standing, not seated. Suited. Left hand holding a blue-bound book. Right arm raised, index finger extended forward. Painted rather than left as bare stone or bronze. Garlanded. Set on a plinth with a plaque.

Nobody enforces this. There is no brand guardian, no licensing body, no approved supplier. Statues are commissioned by local committees, paid for by subscription, cast in small regional workshops from moulds copied off other statues. The consistency is produced entirely by copying — a distributed system that holds together better than most centrally policed ones, and the strongest argument in this atlas that identity is maintained by users, not owners.

The grille is the tell. Many statues are enclosed in a locked cage or fenced plinth, because they are attacked: spectacles broken, garlands removed, the statue tarred, painted or beheaded. Vandalism is unusually informative here. It proves the mark is working on the people it was aimed at. Nicolas Jaoul's fieldwork reads these statues as territorial claims — a piece of public ground occupied by a suited Dalit body, permanently — which is precisely why the ground gets contested.

1972 · The Panthers, imported wholesale

The Dalit Panthers were founded in Bombay in 1972 by Namdeo Dhasal, J. V. Pawar and Raja Dhale, and named — explicitly, without euphemism — after the Black Panther Party. What they imported was not iconography so much as format: the party name as a threat, the numbered manifesto (1973), the declarative stance, the assumption that a small organisation should publish like a government.

The medium was cheap and fast. Marathi Dalit little magazines — Gangadhar Pantawane's Asmitadarsh from the late 1960s onward — were cyclostyled: typed onto a wax stencil, cranked off a drum, stapled. Dhasal's Golpitha (1972) brought Bombay's red-light district into Marathi poetry in language the literary establishment could not process. The typography is crude because the duplicator was crude, and the crudeness became a register in its own right — the only place in this movement where roughness is allowed.

The line continues into the Bhim Geet trade: devotional and militant songs about Ambedkar sold on cassette and later CD, with covers assembled by small-town studios from the same three ingredients — blue field, wheel, portrait.

Now · Blue cap, blue LED, blue hashtag

The Bhim Army, founded in Saharanpur in 2015, added exactly one element: a blue cap, worn as a uniform by young men in a movement that had previously distributed itself through statues and flags. It is the cheapest possible wearable, and it photographs in a crowd.

Meanwhile the statues acquired blue LED haloes and floodlighting, the salute became a hashtag, the stencil became a sticker, and in 2021 Jai Bhim became the title of a Tamil feature film. The portrait itself is now, by most counts, the most reproduced political image in India after Gandhi's — achieved without a state, a party in power, or a single art director.

§ 04 — Artifacts

Four objects, drawn as specifications

Read these the way the movement asks to be read: as drawings with tolerances, not as devotional images. Where a component varies in practice, it is labelled as variable.

Every drawing here is constructed from published descriptions and specifications, not traced from photographs. The portrait schedule and the statue elevation are generalisations of a type, not renderings of any one object: no central specification for either has ever existed, which is the finding rather than a caveat. Colour values for the Buddhist flag follow common published usage; manufactured flags vary.
