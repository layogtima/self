The Dravidian Movement

No. 03South Asia · Tamil Nadu1925 — present

The only political design system on earth art-directed by screenwriters and executed by cinema hoarding painters. Two registers that never reconciled: the matte black shirt of Periyar's rationalists, and sixty feet of airbrushed plywood erected outside a theatre, garlanded, and bathed in milk.

The anatomy

A palette assembled from two industries

Half of this system came out of a newspaper office in Erode running on the cheapest paper available. The other half came out of the enamel tins used to paint film banners in Kodambakkam. Nothing in it was specified by a designer; all of it was specified by a supplier — and both suppliers sold exactly one red.

Lettering as a territorial claim

In a state whose politics is a two-generation argument about which script the government may print in, type is never neutral. Tamil display lettering here is deliberately fat, wide and loop-heavy — built to be read at sixty feet from a moving bus, and built so that no Devanagari headline nearby can out-shout it.

The marks

Four marks, two of them made by painters

How it worked

Most movements in this atlas designed a poster and hoped it would be seen. The Dravidian movement did something stranger: it took over a film industry, and let the film industry design the politics. Dialogue writers became chief ministers. Leading men became parties. The publicity department of Tamil cinema became, for forty years, the graphic arm of the Tamil state.

It produced two visual registers that sit badly together and are both authentically its own. One is austere: black shirt, red disc, cheap Tamil newsprint, no ornament, no gods. The other is enormous: sixty feet of airbrushed enamel with a gold border and a garland. The movement's whole design history is the argument between them.

1925 · Black, chosen as the anti-colour

E.V. Ramasamy — Periyar — walked out of the Indian National Congress in 1925 and started the Self-Respect Movement, சுயமரியாதை இயக்கம். The same year he launched the Tamil weekly Kudi Arasu. He had already spent 1924–25 at the Vaikom satyagraha, arguing about which castes could walk on which road.

The clothing decision came with the politics. Cadre wore black shirts; the flag was a black field with a red circle. Every other Indian political colour of the period was a claim to sanctity — saffron for renunciation, white for ritual purity, green for a community. Black is a refusal of the whole vocabulary. It has no auspicious reading in the tradition it is arguing with, which is exactly why it was picked.

It is also, incidentally, the cheapest reliable dye and the only shirt colour that still looks deliberate after three days of marching. Self-Respect weddings were conducted with no priest, no fire and no Sanskrit; the visual equivalent was a uniform with nothing on it. Periyar went further than symbolism in the 1950s, staging public image-breaking campaigns and burning pictures of Rama. The design was polite. The politics was not.

1937 · When a script becomes a position

In 1937 the Madras Presidency government made Hindi compulsory in schools. The agitation that followed ran to 1940, produced deaths in custody, and gave the movement its most durable slogan: இந்தி ஒழிக — down with Hindi. In 1965, when Hindi was to become the sole official language of the Union, it happened again, larger and bloodier. Two years later the Congress lost Tamil Nadu and has never governed it since.

What makes this a design story rather than a language story is what the movement then did to the writing system itself. Periyar had argued for years that the Tamil script was needlessly hard to set: certain vowel signs fuse with their consonant into a single irregular composite glyph, which means a compositor needs a separate sort for each fusion. His proposal was to regularise them — write the consonant, then the sign.

In 1978 the Tamil Nadu government, under Karunanidhi, adopted a set of reformed letterforms along those lines. Compare a Tamil schoolbook printed in 1970 with one printed in 1980 and the difference is visible on every page. It is one of very few occasions anywhere on which a political movement got as far as editing the letters.

Every other movement in this atlas printed its message. This one cast it, shot it, released it on a Friday, and won an election with it.

1949 · A sun you could vote for

C.N. Annadurai split from Periyar in 1949 and founded the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam. Annadurai was a playwright and an orator before he was anything else, and the DMK's visual choices are a writer's choices: the rising sun, உதயசூரியன், allotted as its Election Commission symbol, and black and red carried over from the parent organisation.

The Indian ballot symbol system — built so that a voter who cannot read can still vote unaided — turns every party mark into a pictogram with a legal specification. It is a brutal design brief: one flat shape, no colour on the ballot paper, legible at thumbnail size, and it must not resemble any other party's shape. The rising sun passes on every count. It is also the only symbol in Indian politics that arrives pre-loaded with a tense: not power, not victory — morning. A thing that is about to happen.

Flag specifics in this family of parties are genuinely contested and often misreported, so the recreations below keep the ballot symbol and the black-and-red field as separate, separately-labelled objects rather than asserting one construction.

1952 · The screenplay as party manifesto

Parasakthi was released in 1952. M. Karunanidhi wrote it; Sivaji Ganesan made his debut in it; the dialogue — alliterative, Sanskrit-purged, built in long rhetorical waves — was the point. Audiences bought tickets to hear a political style. Karunanidhi became chief minister in 1969 and stayed a working screenwriter.

This is the mechanism that makes the Dravidian movement unlike anything else here. Rhetoric written for the screen transferred, unaltered, to the public platform, and from there to the poster and the wall. Bernard Bate's work on Tamil oratory tracks the same cadences moving between all three. The medium chain runs film → stage → wall, not the other way round.

The consequence was a personality cult built by an industry that already knew how to manufacture one. M.G. Ramachandran's fan clubs became a welfare network and then a party; M.S.S. Pandian's The Image Trap is the standard account of how a screen persona of the incorruptible poor man's champion was converted into an electoral asset. A movement founded on rationalism and the demolition of idols ended up with the largest idols in the country, made of plywood.

1970s · Sixty feet of plywood, then nothing

The cut-out is the movement's signature object and its most technically serious one. Canvas or plywood on a tubular scaffold, thirty to sixty feet, painted flat on the ground in enamel by the same men who painted the film banners over the theatre entrance, then raised, garlanded, and on occasion bathed in milk.

The craft problem is foreshortening. A face painted correctly on a flat panel looks wrong once you are standing under it at a twelve-degree sightline, so the painters corrected upward: heads and upper bodies enlarged against the legs, features pushed up the skull, gradients graded so the cheek still turns when lit from below by a sodium lamp. The skin was airbrushed; the border was gold; the edge was cut with a jigsaw and left hard, because a hard edge against the sky reads from further away than a soft one.

Between them, the cut-out and the wall did all the distribution. Cadre-painted enamel Tamil lettering on compound walls — red on limewash, or white and yellow on black — was a printing system with no press, no cost and no possibility of reproduction: every wall a unique impression, executed unpaid overnight by a man with a bucket and a two-inch brush and a set of display conventions he had never seen written down.

Digital flex printing arrived in the late 1990s and finished both trades inside a decade. A vinyl banner is cheaper, faster, weatherproof and dead. Preminda Jacob's Celluloid Deities documents the studios in the last years of hand painting. The style survives as a filter; the trade does not.

  • Bernard Bate, Tamil Oratory and the Dravidian Aesthetic: Democratic Practice in South India (2009) — the platform, the banner and the sentence, treated as one form.
  • Stephen Putnam Hughes, essays on early Tamil cinema exhibition, publicity and audiences — for how the film industry's promotional apparatus was built before politics borrowed it.
  • Government of Tamil Nadu, 1978 — the adoption of reformed Tamil letterforms. Legible in any comparison of Tamil schoolbooks printed either side of that date.
  • Periyar E.V. Ramasamy, Kudi Arasu (from 1925) and Viduthalai (from 1935) — the movement's own printed record; runs held at the Periyar Library, Chennai and in Tamil digital library collections.
  • Roja Muthiah Research Library, Chennai — film publicity, posters, song books and ephemera; the single best archive for this material.
